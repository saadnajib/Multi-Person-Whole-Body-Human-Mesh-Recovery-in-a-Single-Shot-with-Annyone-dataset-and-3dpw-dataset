\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{ch:background}

My network predicts bodies in one body model, Anny, but the real photos it trains on, 3DPW, are labelled in another,
SMPL. I have to convert those labels, so I describe both models before the data. The new parts of my work are listed in Section~\ref{sec:intro-contrib}.

\section{The body models SMPL and Anny}
\label{sec:bg-bodymodels}

A body model turns a few numbers into a 3D mesh of a person, a surface of points (vertices) joined into triangles.
The pose $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ says how the body bends and the shape $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ how it is built. The
function $M(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}) \rightarrow \mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}$ is
differentiable, so a network can be trained through it. The shape deforms a template mesh in its rest pose. Then the
pose turns a skeleton of bones, and the skin follows by \emph{linear blend skinning} (LBS):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_i' \;=\; \sum_{k} w_{ik}\, \mathbf{G}_k(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})\, \mathbf{v}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta}),
  \label{eq:bg-lbs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ is the shaped rest vertex, $w_{ik}$ the skinning weight of bone $k$ and
$\mathbf{G}_k$ the bone's world transform, found by chaining the rotations of all bones from the root down to $k$. In
words, every vertex moves with the bones it is attached to, mixed by its weights. Because the transforms chain, a wrong
rotation high up in the chain of bones moves everything below it. For example, in Appendix~\ref{ch:diagnostics} two
neck bones are each turned by half a turn. The head still points the right way, but the skin between them is twisted.

\paragraph{The SMPL family.}
SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl}, the body model of most earlier work and of the 3DPW labels, has \num{\BgSmplVertices}
vertices and \BgSmplJoints{} joints. Its shape space was learned from registered scans of adults as principal
components, and most methods use the first \BgSmplBetas{} coefficients, called betas. These are statistical
directions, not named properties: no coefficient means ``older'' or ``more muscular''. Because the scans are of adults,
SMPL represents child proportions poorly. SMIL~\cite{hesse2018smil} is a separate infant model, and
AGORA~\cite{patel2021agora} and BEV~\cite{sun2022bev} blend the adult template towards an infant one with one extra
coefficient. SMPL-H~\cite{romero2017mano} adds MANO hands with \BgManoHandJoints{} joints each, and
SMPL-X~\cite{pavlakos2019smplx} also adds a face, for \BgSmplxJoints{} joints on \num{\BgSmplxVertices} vertices. SMPL
is released under a research licence. The assets, datasets and trained weights each have their own terms, which have to
be checked separately. Multi-HMR, the network my work starts from, was published for
SMPL-X~\cite{baradel2024multihmr}. NAVER's later Anny release replaced its output layer, and my teacher starts from that
release's backbone weights and uses its own heads (Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}).

\paragraph{Anny.}
Anny~\cite{bregier2025anny} is a body model by NAVER LABS Europe. It is built from the open MakeHuman
project~\cite{makehuman}, an artist-made human model, and not from scans. Its code is under the Apache~2.0 licence and
its assets under CC0~\cite{naver2025annycode}. I use the full rig of \BgAnnyBones{} bones, because the AnnyOne labels
are written for it. The Anny package loads a pruned rig of \BgAnnyDefaultRigBones{} bones by default, and that rig does
not fit the checkpoint or the labels. So the package, its rig and its mesh have to be the ones the checkpoint and the
labels were made with. Pose is one rigid transform per bone, applied by Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-lbs}. Of the bones, \BgAnnyHelperBones{} are helper bones (toes, breasts, small
face bones) whose rotation an image cannot show. I keep them at rest, which leaves \BgAnnyActiveBones{} predicted
bones. The neck has three stacked bones, \texttt{neck01} to \texttt{neck03}, followed by \texttt{head}.

Shape is set by \BgAnnyPhenotypes{} \emph{phenotype} values between 0 and 1, such as \texttt{gender}, \texttt{age},
\texttt{weight} and three blend weights \texttt{african}, \texttt{asian} and \texttt{caucasian}
(all in Table~\ref{tab:body-models}). Each value drives blend shapes, artist-made
offsets of the rest-mesh vertices. Because the values have names, a predicted body can be read as ``older'' or
``heavier''. Gender 0 is the male end and 1 the female end, with rest statures of
\BgStatureGenderZero\,m and \BgStatureGenderOne\,m. Age blends five anchor bodies spaced evenly from
$-\tfrac{1}{3}$ to 1. Age 0 is the baby, \BgAgeAnchorChild{} the child, \BgAgeAnchorYoung{} the
young adult and 1 the old adult. The newborn would need an age below 0, which a network output squashed into $(0,1)$
never gives.

Figure~\ref{fig:bg-age-sweep} shows what age does. At the mean AnnyOne shape, moving age from 0 to 1 raises the
skeletal height from \BgSweepHeightAgeZero\,m to \BgSweepHeightAgeOne\,m (\BgSweepHeightAgeHalf\,m at 0.5), so most of
the change happens below about 0.5. A short body alone does not make a child. At age 0.6 the smallest reachable body is
\BgSmallestAdultStature\,m tall and still has adult proportions. What marks a child is narrow shoulders relative to the
head. The shoulder-to-head width ratio is \BgShoulderHeadChild{} at age 0.1 against \BgShoulderHeadAdult{} at 0.6, and
only a low age produces it. I never convert ages to years, because three scales
in the code disagree. I call a person \emph{child-shaped} if the age value is below about \BgChildShapeAge.

\paragraph{SMPL and Anny side by side.}
Table~\ref{tab:body-models} and Figure~\ref{fig:body-models} put the two models side by side. They differ most in
the skeleton (\BgSmplJoints{} joints against \BgAnnyBones{} bones) and in the shape (\BgSmplBetas{} unnamed
coefficients against \BgAnnyPhenotypes{} named phenotypes). SMPL stores the pose as one axis-angle rotation per joint,
a vector of three numbers whose direction is the axis and whose length is the angle. My network gives each Anny rotation as \MethodRotDims{} numbers (the 6D
form), which are turned into a valid rotation matrix.

The only dataset with Anny labels, AnnyOne, is synthetic, and the real photos I have, 3DPW
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-labels}), come with SMPL labels. This is why Chapter~\ref{ch:data} converts 3DPW.

\paragraph{AnnyOne.}
AnnyOne is NAVER's synthetic dataset for Anny: computer-rendered scenes of several people, each with exact Anny
labels (all bone transforms, all phenotypes and the camera). Its authors describe more than
\num{\BgAnnyOnePublished} images~\cite{bravo2026annyfit}; the release copy I train on holds \num{\BgAnnyOneImages}. It is my only source of complete Anny labels, shape included (Section~\ref{sec:data-annyone}).

\section{Image features and attention}
\label{sec:bg-transformer}

A Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} cuts an image into square patches and turns each into a vector,
a token. Attention mixes information between tokens through queries $\mathbf Q$, keys $\mathbf K_a$
and values $\mathbf V_a$:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf Q,\mathbf K_a,\mathbf V_a)
 =\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf Q\mathbf K_a^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf V_a ,
 \label{eq:bg-attention}
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the length of a key. In words, each query takes an average of the values, weighted by how well it matches
each key. The subscript $a$ keeps these keys apart from the camera intrinsics $\mathbf K$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:method}). Multi-HMR uses self-attention between image patches and cross-attention, where a person
query reads from all image tokens. Both backbones are DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} ViTs as NAVER
released them with Multi-HMR, a ViT-L for the teacher and a ViT-B for the student. The ViT-B produces shorter token
vectors than the ViT-L, so the student needs a projector (Section~\ref{sec:bg-kd}).

\section{Single-person and multi-person mesh recovery}
\label{sec:bg-hmr}

Human mesh recovery (HMR) estimates the mesh of a body model from an image.

\paragraph{Regression and fitting.}
HMR~\cite{kanazawa2018hmr} regressed body-model parameters for one cropped person with a convolutional encoder, an
iterative regressor and an adversarial pose prior (a second network that tells real poses from impossible ones).
HMR~2.0~\cite{goel2023humans4d} replaced the encoder by a ViT and showed that a large ViT trained on a broad mix of
datasets works well without further tricks. Multi-HMR uses the same recipe, and so does my model. The network outputs
the parameters directly, with no optimisation at test time. SMPLify~\cite{bogo2016smplify} goes the other way and fits
SMPL to 2D keypoints by optimisation, and SPIN~\cite{kolotouros2019spin} puts such a fit inside the training loop. My
3DPW converter (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}) is an optimisation of the SMPLify kind, but in 3D, between two body models, and
run once offline.

\paragraph{Single-shot multi-person methods.}
Two-stage methods run a person detector and then a single-person network on each crop, so the cost grows with the
number of people. ROMP~\cite{sun2021romp} does both in one pass (single-shot): it predicts a heatmap
of body centres and reads the body parameters at the detected centres. Multi-HMR~\cite{baradel2024multihmr}, the
architecture of my work, keeps this idea. Its ViT turns the image into one token per
$\BgPatch{\times}\BgPatch$-pixel patch, which gives a $\BgTokenGrid{\times}\BgTokenGrid$ grid of tokens for the
$\BgInputRes{\times}\BgInputRes$-pixel input. Each token
scores whether a person's primary keypoint, the head joint in the released code~\cite{naver2024multihmrcode}, lies in
it. High-scoring tokens become the queries of the \emph{Human Perception Head} (HPH),
which attends to all image tokens and predicts the body parameters and the distance of the person's head joint from the
camera. I call this head joint the anchor. It is at the centre of the depth defect in Appendix~\ref{ch:diagnostics}.
Section~\ref{sec:method-architecture} gives the details.

The released SMPL-X checkpoints reach a PVE (mean vertex distance, Section~\ref{sec:method-eval}) of
\BgMhrLPVE\,mm on the 3DPW test set with ViT-L in \BgMhrLMs\,ms, and \BgMhrBPVE\,mm with ViT-B in \BgMhrBMs\,ms
on a V100 GPU~\cite{naver2024multihmrcode}. The ViT-B is as accurate and faster, which is why my smaller model is a
ViT-B student. These are SMPL-X errors and cannot be compared with the Anny-space errors of this thesis.

\paragraph{BEV and Relative Human.}
BEV~\cite{sun2022bev} extends ROMP in two ways: it also predicts how far each person stands from the camera, and its
body model has an age parameter. Its authors collected Relative Human, real photos in which every person has an age
group (adult, teenager, kid, baby), a depth layer and keypoints, but no 3D body. It is the only real data with age
labels I could use, and I use it for evaluation only (Section~\ref{sec:data-rh}).

\paragraph{Multi-HMR-Anny and Anny-Fit.}
NAVER has released a Multi-HMR checkpoint that predicts Anny, trained on BEDLAM, AnnyOne and further real
images~\cite{naver2024multihmrcode}. So a multi-person network for Anny already exists, and I do not claim to be the
first. This checkpoint, Multi-HMR-Anny, gives my teacher's backbone weights and is the baseline I compare against.
On the same backbone its decoder has \BgNaverHeadLayers{}
cross-attention blocks and \BgNaverHeadParams\,M parameters, against \BgOurHeadLayers{} blocks and
\BgOurHeadParams\,M in mine. It was trained on AnnyOne, so it may have seen my AnnyOne test images
during training (Section~\ref{sec:res-baseline}). Anny-Fit~\cite{bravo2026annyfit}, by the same group, refines such predictions per
image by optimisation and adapts them to children at test time. My model stays a single forward pass. I did not use
Anny-Fit. For future work on children it would be the first method to compare against.

\section{Synthetic training data}
\label{sec:bg-synthetic}

AGORA~\cite{patel2021agora} renders scanned people with SMPL-X fits into realistic scenes, and
BEDLAM~\cite{black2023bedlam} showed that synthetic images alone can train competitive networks. AnnyOne does the same
for Anny. Synthetic labels are exact and complete, but the images look different from photos, so a network trained on them
can work less well on real photos. In my experiments the shape predictions followed the labels on the
synthetic holdout, the AnnyOne images I keep out of training, but collapsed towards the training average on real photos
(Section~\ref{sec:res-rh}). This is why Final, my teacher, mixes \BgMixReal\,\% real images into every batch, and why
those images need Anny labels.

\section{Real-data labels for a new body model}
\label{sec:bg-labels}

3DPW~\cite{vonmarcard2018_3dpw} is the standard real-world test set for mesh recovery: \BgThreeDPWSequences{} video
sequences with SMPL labels for one or two actors each. SMPL and Anny have different skeletons and different shape
spaces, so I have to refit the labels in Anny. The SMPL-X code transfers fits between SMPL-family models through a
vertex correspondence~\cite{pavlakos2019smplx}, and the Anny package has an inverter that fits Anny to a target
mesh~\cite{naver2025annycode}. Neither turns a 3DPW label into an Anny label directly, so I wrote my own converter
(Section~\ref{sec:data-converter}). My fit uses only joint positions, so it can only recover what the
joint positions fix. Section~\ref{sec:data-limits} lists what the converted labels can and cannot test. SHAPY~\cite{choutas2022shapy} instead
supervises shape on real images with body measurements and word attributes. Anny's named phenotypes would suit such
labels, but I collected none.

\section{Knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:bg-kd}

A large network is accurate but slow. Knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} trains a small \emph{student}
to imitate a large, trained \emph{teacher}, originally by matching its class probabilities softened by a
temperature. FitNets~\cite{romero2015fitnets} let the student also match an intermediate
representation of the teacher through a small learned regressor. DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} distilled its smaller
ViTs from its largest one, so a DINOv2 ViT-B is a good starting point for a student. My Student uses both forms and
matches the teacher's image features and outputs (Section~\ref{sec:method-kd}). The
Student has \BgStudentParams\,M parameters and Final has \BgFinalParams\,M. What I want to know is whether the Student
keeps at least \BgNinetyFive\,\% of Final's accuracy on both test sets.

\paragraph{The projector.}
The student starts with a copy of the teacher's prediction heads. Its features have \BgProjIn{} channels, but the
teacher's features and the copied heads use \BgProjOut. A projector, one linear layer, converts one width into the
other, so the student's features can be compared with the teacher's and fed into the copied heads. Because the heads
read its output, the projector stays in the deployed student.

\paragraph{The temperature.}
After the projector, each patch token is a list of \BgProjOut{} channel values. Teacher and student tokens are
compared in three steps. First, the values are divided by the temperature $T$. Then a softmax turns them into
positive weights that add up to 1. Finally, the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence measures how differently teacher and
student spread these weights. It is zero when they are equal.

A larger $T$ makes the weights softer. For example, two channels with weights [\BgTempExampleHigh, \BgTempExampleLow] at $T=1$ get about
[\BgTempExampleSoftHigh, \BgTempExampleSoftLow] at $T=\MethodKDTemp$ (an illustration, not a measurement).
Softer weights let the student also learn from the smaller channel responses. The weights describe feature patterns,
not probabilities of body parts. Dividing by $T$ shrinks the gradients by about $T^2$, so the loss is multiplied by $T^2$
to keep the gradient size comparable across temperatures~\cite{hinton2015distilling}.
% Source of the T^2 factor: distill.py:16-19 (docstring) and :97 (kl * T**2).

The output distillation is a weighted L1 loss. It takes the absolute differences between the student's and the
teacher's rotations, phenotypes and distances and weights each term as in the supervised loss. It has no temperature.
% Source of the weights: train.py:526-539 (alpha_rotmat, alpha_shape, alpha_dist); final deck slide 4.
The reported Student uses $T=\MethodKDTemp$. Section~\ref{sec:res-kd-temperature} tries other values.

\section{Temporal models for video}
\label{sec:bg-temporal}

Most video methods refine the per-frame features of an image network: HMMR~\cite{kanazawa2019hmmr} with a
temporal convolution, VIBE~\cite{kocabas2020vibe} with a recurrent network and a motion discriminator tied to SMPL, and
TCMR~\cite{choi2021tcmr} with branches that predict the current frame from its neighbours to reduce jitter (small
frame-to-frame shaking). MPS-Net~\cite{wei2022mpsnet} and GLoT~\cite{shen2023glot} use attention over
frames. SmoothNet~\cite{zeng2022smoothnet} is a learned network that smooths the output poses of any estimator over a
window of frames. PHALP~\cite{rajasegaran2022phalp} and 4DHumans~\cite{goel2023humans4d} track
people through video, the first step of any multi-person video method, and WHAM~\cite{shin2024wham} places the motion
in world coordinates.

My student sees one frame at a time, so the body it predicts can shake or change size between frames. I keep it
frame-wise and add two steps at inference time, without retraining (Sections~\ref{sec:method-video}
and~\ref{sec:method-temporal}). First, an
exponential moving average blends each tracked person's new bone rotations, phenotypes and position with the earlier
ones. Like SmoothNet, it smooths outputs without image features. But it is a fixed formula, not a learned network, it is
causal (only the current and earlier frames count), and it works on body-model parameters. Second, the pipeline moves the
body's position and depth so that they match 2D landmarks from a separate detector. The learned methods above remain future
work.
