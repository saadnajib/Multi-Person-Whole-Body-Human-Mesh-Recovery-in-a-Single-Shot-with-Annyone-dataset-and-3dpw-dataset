\chapter{Supporting diagnostic experiments}
\label{ch:diagnostics}

When I rendered the predictions of Base, three things were visibly wrong. The necks were thin and twisted, the
fingers were bent into claws, and children received adult bodies. Before I changed any training setting, I tested each
idea I had about the causes on its own, and several of these ideas turned out to be wrong. Chapter~\ref{ch:method}
only defines the two corrections that came out of this work; the evidence for them is in this appendix. Unless I say
otherwise, every number below comes from Base on the \DiagHoldoutImages-image AnnyOne holdout in setting V1. Each
variant was trained once, without a fixed seed, so I compare every difference with the run-to-run variation measured
in Section~\ref{sec:diag-noise}.

In Figure~\ref{fig:diag-neck-base} (left) Base draws each neck as a thin stalk pinched under the head. The fingers
curled into claws, so every real-photo demo set them to rest pose. A standing boy in a demo photo received an adult
body (Figure~\ref{fig:real-photos}).

In my first draft I had written down a cause for each failure without testing it. For the necks I blamed the
skinning of the stacked neck bones, which I called ``a property of the rig'' (the rig is the skeleton that moves the
mesh). For the fingers I blamed their small size in the image. For the children I blamed an age the network had never
learned, and as evidence I pointed to five small people in the demo photos who were predicted at adult ages
(\DiagSmallPeopleAgeLow--\DiagSmallPeopleAgeHigh). The neck cause turned out to be wrong, and the small size of the
fingers explains only a small part of their error. The child evidence was wrong too. Four of the five small people
were adults who crouched, sat or were cut off by the frame, and their predicted height phenotype was
\DiagSmallPeopleHeightLow--\DiagSmallPeopleHeightHigh. Only the boy was a child. He received age \DiagBoyAge{} and
height \DiagBoyHeight, which is an adult body. The number that measures the thin necks is the neck girth in
Table~\ref{tab:neck-bones}, the predicted neck circumference divided by the true one.

Before I blamed the network, I checked the parts around it: the labels, the body model, its rig and the path of the
gradient from the loss back to the weights. Table~\ref{tab:diag-checks} lists the six checks. Check~1, for example,
decodes the labels and draws them over the image without any network, so a wrong label or a wrong camera would show up
there (Figure~\ref{fig:diag-gt-decode}). The rig, the label decoding and the gradient path were all fine, so the cause
had to lie in what the training targets and the losses asked the network to do.

I also looked at how the total loss was split at the end of Base's training. The shape term made up
\DiagLossShareShape\,\% of it, the translation term \DiagLossShareTransl\,\% and the distance term
\DiagLossShareDist\,\%. This matters for placement. The 3D terms move both pelvises to the same point before they
compare the bodies, so they cannot tell whether a person stands at the right distance. Only the distance and
translation terms can, and before the depth fix the two together were only \DiagLossShareDepthTerms\,\% of the loss.
Two details in the log are easy to misread. The log calls the detection term \texttt{bce}, but it is the focal loss of
CornerNet~\cite{law2018cornernet} and not a binary cross-entropy. And the neck and hand boost
(Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights}) only moves rotation-loss weight from some bones to others; it adds no loss on top.

The overfit run of check~6 gave two warnings. The rotation loss barely moved while the images were memorised
(\DiagOverfitRotFirst{} $\to$ \DiagOverfitRotLast), and the translation and 2D terms rose (\DiagOverfitTranslFirst{}
$\to$ \DiagOverfitTranslLast, \DiagOverfitTwoDFirst{} $\to$ \DiagOverfitTwoDLast, first against last five epochs),
possibly a first sign of the depth bug. That evidence is weak: the values are single-batch prints, and the half-run
averages fall (\DiagOverfitTranslHalfA{} $\to$ \DiagOverfitTranslHalfB,
\DiagOverfitTwoDHalfA{} $\to$ \DiagOverfitTwoDHalfB).

Two faults were present that these checks could not find. The first was a depth target built at a different joint
than the one the mesh is placed by. The checks compare pelvis-centred quantities, so a placement error does not show
in them. The second was a bone turned the wrong way by the same amount in every person. The checks averaged bone
errors by group, so one bad bone disappeared in the mean of its group.

\input{tables/T08_depth_runs}

Base places every person too far from the camera (Table~\ref{tab:diag-placement}, Figure~\ref{fig:depth-scatter}),
and the people nearest to the camera are pushed back the most. The predicted skeleton has the right height, so a body
that is placed too far away is drawn at \DiagSizeBase{} of its true size in the image. PVE does not notice any of
this, because it moves the predicted pelvis onto the true pelvis before it measures.

I found the cause in the code. The mesh is placed in the image by its head joint, but the anchor targets had been
built at the pelvis (Section~\ref{sec:method-depth}). So the network was asked to put the head where the pelvis is. A
debug print on one batch showed anchor offsets of \DiagOffsetMin{} to \DiagOffsetMax{} cells of the token grid, where
the legal range is $\pm$0.5 of a cell. I did not test on its own why this pushes people away, but the likely reason is
the following. When the mesh hangs from a head that is in the wrong place, the 2D terms can only reduce the mismatch
by drawing the body smaller in the image, and a smaller body means a body farther away. The direct depth terms did not
hold against this, and the mismatch is largest for people near the camera.

The fix is to build the anchor targets at the head, the joint the mesh is placed by. Table~\ref{tab:depth-runs}
compares runs with this fix against control runs that have the same training schedule but keep the pelvis targets.
All five fix runs reach a root error (the 3D distance between predicted and true pelvis) of
\DiagRootErrFixLow--\DiagRootErrFixHigh\,m, while both controls stay at \DiagRootErrCtrlLow--\DiagRootErrCtrlHigh\,m.
The drawn size rises from \DiagSizeCtrlLow--\DiagSizeCtrlHigh{} to \DiagSizeFixLow--\DiagSizeFixHigh, and the head
lands within \DiagHeadPxFixLow--\DiagHeadPxFixHigh{} pixels of its true position instead of
\DiagHeadPxCtrlLow--\DiagHeadPxCtrlHigh. Every fix run lies below every control, far outside the run-to-run
variation. Two other checkpoints trained with pelvis targets carry the bug too. It stayed hidden for so long because
PVE aligns the pelvis first and the real-photo demos place each body from its detection box.

The fix did not give correct depth. It moved the intercept and the body size, but barely the slope of predicted
against true depth (\DiagSlopeSwitch{} with the switch alone, \DiagSlopeStrongLow--\DiagSlopeStrongHigh{} with a
stronger depth loss, against \DiagDepthSlopeBase{} for Base). Predictions are still pulled toward
\DiagDepthPullLow--\DiagDepthPullHigh\,m, for a reason I have not found. The pelvis 2D error rose to
\DiagPelvisPxFixLow--\DiagPelvisPxFixHigh{} pixels. The likely reason is that the head is now pinned in place while
the body is still drawn too small, which I did not check. Every fix run is also worse than its control, by
+\DiagFixCostPVELow{} to +\DiagFixCostPVEHigh\,mm PVE and $-$\DiagFixCostRecallLow{} to $-$\DiagFixCostRecallHigh{}
recall points. Each of these gaps on its own is within the run-to-run variation, but all of them go in the same
direction, so the fix most likely costs some accuracy. I had written a success rule before the runs (PVE $\le$
\DiagRulePVE\,mm, recall $\ge$ \DiagRuleRecall\,\%). By that rule every run failed, and so did the controls.

\section{A per-bone dump of the neck}
\label{sec:diag-neck}

\input{tables/T09_neck}

My earlier neck numbers were group means (\DiagGroupMeanNeck{} and \DiagGroupMeanHead\,deg) that hid which bone was
wrong, so I dumped the error of every bone of the neck chain (Table~\ref{tab:neck-bones}, first column). neck02 is
\DiagDumpNeckTwo\,deg wrong, a half-turn about its own axis (twist \DiagDumpNeckTwoTwist, swing
\DiagDumpNeckTwoSwing\,deg), and the head bone \DiagDumpHead\,deg. neck03 is right relative to its parent
(\DiagDumpNeckThree\,deg) but \DiagDumpNeckThreeGlobal\,deg off in the camera frame. The two half-turns cancel, so the
head points the right way (\DiagDumpHeadGlobal\,deg), but the skin between them is twisted thin, which is what the
low girth shows.

The same flip is present in all eight checkpoints I dumped from the ViT-L line that leads to Base and continues from
it: L-stages 2 to 4, Base, Mixed-L and three short fine-tunes of Base (Table~\ref{tab:model-names}). In all of them
neck02 is off by \DiagNeckTwoRangeLow--\DiagNeckTwoRangeHigh\,deg and the girth is
\DiagGirthRangeLow--\DiagGirthRangeHigh. The flip is already there in L-stage 2, before the helper mask (helper bones
held at rest), the neck boost and the shape weight of 20 were added, so none of these caused it. One of the three
fine-tunes is the 10-epoch depth fix with $\times$5 weight from Table~\ref{tab:depth-runs}, and it keeps the flip too,
so the depth bug is not the cause of the neck either. Fresh-heads-L is a separate ViT-L run that started from the
Multi-HMR-Anny backbone with new heads and saw 3DPW from the first step, without the neck and hand weight. Its neck
is normal (girth \DiagFreshGirth), but other bones are flipped instead: two bones in the left arm whose errors cancel
each other, and three metacarpals.

So the explanation in my draft was wrong. The rig itself is fine, because it passes all \DiagPerturbations{}
single-joint moves, and the rest pose, in which every rotation is the identity, cannot show a flip at all. Changing
the loss did not remove the flip either. The neck boost ($\times$\MethodBoostOn{} for the boosted bones after
renormalisation) left it unchanged. A geodesic loss, which penalises the angle between the predicted and the true
rotation, moved neck02 only from \DiagNeckTwoBase{} to \DiagGeodesicNeckTwo\,deg. The likely reason is that neck02 and
the head have to turn back together, which I did not test. For this reason I corrected the output instead, with one
constant rotation per flipped bone (Section~\ref{sec:method-bonefix}): \DiagCorrNeckTwo, \DiagCorrHead,
\DiagCorrMetacarpal{} and \DiagCorrFinger\,deg for neck02, head, metacarpal1.L and finger4-1.R.

Without training, the correction restores neck02, girth and neck-skin error. But the head direction worsens to
\DiagHeadDirNoTrain\,deg, above the \DiagHeadDirLimit-degree limit I had set beforehand, likely because the head's
error differs per person. A 2-epoch fine-tune brings it to \DiagHeadDirFineTune\,deg (control \DiagHeadDirCtrlTwo).
This is why I fine-tuned the teacher with the correction already in place. A constant added after training removes
the half-turn that every person shares, but it cannot follow an error that differs from person to person. The
differences in body PVE lie within the run-to-run variation, and Figure~\ref{fig:exp6-compare} shows two more holdout
pairs. This section leaves two questions open. I do not know why the bones flipped in the first place. The network
outputs each rotation as six numbers (the 6D form), and their singular start value would explain the tilted
metacarpal1.L, but not the twist of neck02. I also cannot measure the necks on real photos, because they have no ground
truth, so Figure~\ref{fig:neck-3dpw-bar} can only be judged by eye.

\section{The fingers}
\label{sec:diag-fingers}

\input{tables/T10_fingers}

In Table~\ref{tab:fingers} Base's predicted fingers are \DiagFingersBase\,deg off and fingers at rest
\DiagFingersRest\,deg, so the prediction was worse than none. My first explanation was that the hands are too small
in the image for the network to see the fingers. They are small: at the median a hand spans \DiagHandTokens{} tokens,
the small square patches of the image that the encoder works on, and for \DiagHandUnderTwo\,\% of hands fewer than
two. But when I cropped the image around each person at test time, so that the hands became larger, the finger error
stayed at \DiagFingersZoom\,deg while the body PVE nearly doubled. A region-normalised rotation loss did not help
either. It only moves the hands' share of the rotation loss from about \DiagHandShareBoost{} to
\DiagHandShareRegion\,\%, so the hands are weighted differently but not more strongly. Then the per-bone search on
training images found that metacarpal1.L and finger4-1.R are half-turned, like neck02. Correcting these two bones
roughly halves the finger error, both with and without training.

So about half of the finger error came from two flipped bones, and the small size of the hands explains much less.
There are two things I did not do. I never tried a separate hand-crop branch in the network, and with the correction
in place I did not measure again the error of fingers held at rest pose, the floor I compared with above.
Figure~\ref{fig:fingers-demo} shows the fingers on real photos.

\section{Children and body shape}
\label{sec:diag-children}

\input{tables/T18_shape_head}

Table~\ref{tab:shape-head}(a) is my draft's shape-weight test on 10 holdout images: raising the shape weight from 1 to
20 moved the age bias from \DiagShapeAgeBiasOld{} to \DiagShapeAgeBiasNew{} and the height bias from
\DiagShapeHeightBiasOld{} to \DiagShapeHeightBiasNew. Part~(b) shows that this holds only near the mode, the most
common value. In both tails, the rare low and high values, the predictions move toward the training median. The
holdout has no children, so the young tail is measured on training images.

On Relative Human (Table~\ref{tab:relhuman}) Base reads children as short adults. Kids get a mean age of
\DiagRHKidAge, babies \DiagRHBabyAge{} and adults \DiagRHAdultAge{} (age AUC \DiagRHAUC, where 0.5 is chance). None of
\DiagRHKids{} kids and \DiagRHBabiesBelow{} of \DiagRHBabies{} babies fall below the child threshold; the smallness goes
into height instead. Gender carries no signal (accuracy \DiagRHGenderAcc\,\%). On my demo photos I took the medians
over all \DiagDemoShapeLines{} predicted people, one shape line per detection in the demo log (about 30 distinct
people, nearly all adults). These medians (gender \DiagDemoGenderMedian, age \DiagDemoAgeMedian, height
\DiagDemoHeightMedian) equal the training medians.

The children and the female-looking men are the same failure. When the image gives the shape head only weak cues, it
falls back to the training median. I see two possible reasons, and my evidence cannot tell which one matters more. The
first is the gap between synthetic and real images. The second is the loss itself. The shape outputs go through a
sigmoid and are trained with an L1 loss, and an L1 loss is smallest at the median. The sigmoid also flattens at its
ends: its gradient is 0.25 at an output of 0.5 but only 0.015 at an output of 0.015, so an output near the edge of
the range barely moves during training. On top of that, only \DiagChildShare\,\% of the training people are below
age 0.3. The result looks like a short adult because Anny's child
shape appears only below an age of about 0.4--0.5. For example, the shoulders are \DiagShoulderHeadChild{} head widths
wide at age 0.1 and \DiagShoulderHeadAdult{} at 0.6. So a low height at age 0.6 gives a short adult (at least
\DiagSmallestAdult\,m), and proportions barely matter (leg over stature \DiagProportionsLeg).

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} gives two more views of the shape head: how well each predicted phenotype agrees with the
truth on the holdout (Table~\ref{tab:pheno-corr}), and how well the models rank gender on real photos
(Table~\ref{tab:gender-auc}). For Base, Control and Final the slope of predicted against true value is below 1 for
every phenotype, which is the same pull toward the middle as above. Final follows weight (Pearson $r =
\PhenoRFinalWeight$) far better than muscle ($r = \PhenoRFinalMuscle$). Two things I cannot give here. I cannot say
which of the two reasons above dominates, and I cannot give ages in years, because the code uses three age scales
that disagree with each other.

\section{Pilot noise and restart cost}
\label{sec:diag-noise}

\input{tables/T14_pilot_noise}

Two short pilots that I trained with identical settings differed by \DiagNoisePVE\,mm PVE, \DiagNoiseHead\,deg head
direction and \DiagNoiseRecall{} recall points (Table~\ref{tab:pilot-noise}). The code sets no seed. Fine-tuning Base
for two more epochs with nothing changed also made it worse by about \DiagRestartCost\,mm; I call this the restart
cost. For this reason I compare each pilot with its own control and never with Base directly.

Two runs are not enough for a general threshold, and I computed no bootstrap intervals. So I use these differences as
a rough yardstick and not as a significance test. Every change this appendix builds on is far larger than the
yardstick: the root error of the depth fix, neck02 from \DiagNeckTwoBase{} to \DiagNeckTwoNoTrain\,deg, the girth
from \DiagNeckGirthBase{} to \DiagGirthNoTrain, and the fingers from \DiagFingersBase{} to \DiagFingersFixFT\,deg. A
PVE change of a few millimetres, on the other hand, is smaller than the yardstick. For this reason I cannot say
whether the depth fix and the neck pilot cost PVE or not.

\section{What did not work}
\label{sec:diag-negative}

Six training changes did not fix any of the three failures (Appendix~\ref{app:pilots}, Table~\ref{tab:pilots-all}).
An age-balanced sampler gave no clear answer on the 10 children it could be tested on, who come from the training
images. A global head orientation loss stayed within the spread of two identical controls. The region-normalised
hand loss, the geodesic rotation loss and the neck and hand boost did not remove the flipped bones, as the sections
above show. A $\times$20 depth loss gave no gain over
$\times$5. None of the six touched the faults I found later, a wrong anchor target and flipped bones, which the loss
could not see. The two changes that did work, the head-anchor targets and the bone correction, came out of the checks
in this appendix.
