\chapter{Method}
\label{ch:method}

This chapter describes the network, what it is trained to match, the runs that produced the models of this thesis and
how they are evaluated. Section~\ref{sec:method-arch} gives the task and the architecture, Section~\ref{sec:method-losses}
the losses, and Section~\ref{sec:method-schedule} the training schedule; that section also defines the model names used
in every later chapter. Sections~\ref{sec:method-depth} and~\ref{sec:method-bonefix} describe the two changes that
matter most: the training targets now describe the joint the network actually anchors, and a constant rotation at the
output turns four flipped bones back. Section~\ref{sec:method-real} adds real images, Section~\ref{sec:method-kd} the
smaller student, and Section~\ref{sec:method-eval} the evaluation protocol. Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics} shows how the
two defects were found; Chapter~\ref{ch:results} measures what the changes did.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Multi-HMR for Anny: task, architecture, inputs and outputs}
\label{sec:method-arch}

\subsection{The task}
\label{sec:method-task}

The input is one RGB image $\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ with camera intrinsics
$\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ (estimated from an assumed field of view when they are not known). It shows an
unknown number $n$ of people. For each person $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ the network predicts
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{R}_i &\in SO(3)^{J},\quad J=\MethodNumBones \quad\text{(parent-relative bone rotations)},\\
  \boldsymbol{\phi}_i &\in [0,1]^{P},\quad P=\MethodNumPheno \quad\text{(phenotypes)},\\
  (u_i, v_i, z_i) &\quad\text{(image position and depth of one anchor joint, the head)}.
\end{align}
The Anny body model $M$ \cite{bregier2025anny} turns $(\mathbf{R}_i,\boldsymbol{\phi}_i)$ into mesh vertices and joint
positions. The mesh is then moved so that its anchor joint $a$ sits at the back-projection of the anchor pixel at the
predicted depth:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{V}_i = M_V(\mathbf{R}_i,\boldsymbol{\phi}_i) - M_{J,a}(\mathbf{R}_i,\boldsymbol{\phi}_i) + \mathbf{t}_i,
  \qquad
  \mathbf{t}_i = z_i\,\mathbf{K}^{-1}\,(u_i, v_i, 1)^{\top}.
  \label{eq:method-placement}
\end{equation}
The goal is that $\mathbf{V}_i$ matches the person's true body surface in the camera frame: right pose, right shape,
right place. The network sees the whole image once. It uses no person crops and no other video frames. A second goal,
from requirement 1b, is a much smaller network that keeps the error within 95\% of the large one
(Section~\ref{sec:method-student}).

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:method-architecture}

The network is Multi-HMR \cite{baradel2024multihmr} with its output resized for Anny (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}).
I did not change the backbone, the detection or the cross-attention head. This matters for requirement 1b, which asks
for a model that is not exactly Multi-HMR: the architecture alone does not meet it, and the route to 1b is the smaller
student of Section~\ref{sec:method-kd}.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/tikz/F01_architecture}
  \caption{The network. A $\MethodImgSize$-pixel image passes through a DINOv2 ViT-L backbone ($\MethodTokenGrid\times
  \MethodTokenGrid$ tokens). Every token gets a detection score and a 2D offset for the anchor joint, the head. For
  each detected person a cross-attention head (HPH) predicts $\MethodNumBones\times\MethodRotDims$ rotation values,
  \MethodNumPheno{} phenotypes and a depth; a constant correction turns four flipped bones back; the Anny layer builds
  the body and places it at the predicted distance. Shaded boxes are changed or added in this thesis. The dashed box
  marks the part that distillation replaces (Section~\ref{sec:method-kd}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Backbone.} A DINOv2 ViT-L with patch size \MethodPatchSize{} \cite{oquab2023dinov2} maps the
$\MethodImgSize\times\MethodImgSize$ input to a $\MethodTokenGrid\times\MethodTokenGrid$ grid of
$\num{\MethodNumTokens}$ tokens of width \MethodWidthL. A token covers $\MethodPatchSize\times\MethodPatchSize$ pixels.
AnnyOne renders are $\MethodRenderSize\times\MethodRenderSize$ pixels and are resized to the network input. The backbone weights come from NAVER's Anny checkpoint
(Section~\ref{sec:method-schedule}). The backbone holds \MethodParamsBackboneL\,M of the network's
\MethodParamsTotalL\,M parameters.

\paragraph{Detection.} A two-layer MLP gives every token a score in $(0,1)$: the probability that a person's anchor
joint lies in this token. At inference a $3\times3$ non-maximum suppression and a threshold keep the detected tokens.
A second two-layer MLP predicts, for each detected token, a 2D offset in units of a token, so that the anchor pixel is
$(\text{token index}+0.5+\text{offset})\cdot\MethodPatchSize$. In every model of this thesis the anchor joint is the
head, Anny's bone at the base of the skull (\texttt{--person\_center head}). Section~\ref{sec:method-depth} shows why
this choice matters for the training targets.

\paragraph{Camera rays.} Each token is concatenated with a Fourier embedding of its viewing ray through $\mathbf{K}$
(\MethodCamChannels{} channels). The head therefore knows in which direction of the camera frustum a token looks.

\paragraph{Human Perception Head (HPH).} Each detected token becomes one query. The query is built from the token, its
ray embedding, learned row and column embeddings of its grid position, and constant start values for pose, shape and
camera. A transformer decoder with \MethodHPHDepth{} blocks, \MethodHPHHeads{} heads of size \MethodHPHDimHead, width
\MethodHPHDim{} and MLP width \MethodHPHMlp{} processes all queries of an image together: in each block the queries
attend to each other (self-attention) and then to all image tokens (cross-attention). These are the values in
\texttt{model.py}; the draft of this thesis gave a width of 512 and 16 heads, which was wrong. The decoder's output
token $\mathbf{y}_i$ feeds three linear layers.

\paragraph{Anny head.} The three linear layers are the part that differs from the SMPL-X version of Multi-HMR.
\emph{Pose:} $\MethodNumBones\times\MethodRotDims$ values are added to the identity rotation in the continuous 6D
representation of Zhou et al.\ \cite{zhou2019continuity}; Gram--Schmidt orthonormalisation turns each 6-vector into a
rotation matrix. \emph{Shape:} \MethodNumPheno{} logits pass through a sigmoid, so every phenotype lies in $[0,1]$.
\emph{Depth:} the first of three camera values is a log-depth normalised by the focal length, as in Multi-HMR:
$z = \exp(d\, f/f_n)$, with $f_n$ the focal length of a \MethodFovN$^\circ$ field of view at the input size, clamped to
\MethodMaxDist\,m. The other two camera values are not used.

\paragraph{Helper-bone mask.} A fixed mask marks \MethodActiveBones{} of the \MethodNumBones{} bones as predicted and
forces the other \MethodHelperBones{} (toes, breasts, face and subdivision bones) to the identity,
$\mathbf{R}_{i,j}\leftarrow m_j\mathbf{R}_{i,j}+(1-m_j)\mathbf{I}$. The same mask is applied to the ground truth during
training, so the masked bones add nothing to the loss.

\paragraph{Bone correction.} Right after the Gram--Schmidt step, and before the mask, four bones are multiplied by one
constant rotation each, $\mathbf{R}_b\leftarrow\mathbf{R}_b\mathbf{C}_b$. The correction has no parameters.
Section~\ref{sec:method-bonefix} explains it.

\paragraph{Body-model layer.} The Anny model runs inside the network, in PyTorch, with linear blend skinning. It turns
the rotations and phenotypes into vertices $\mathbf{V}_i$ and joints $\mathbf{J}_i$ and places them with
Equation~\eqref{eq:method-placement}. The 3D and 2D losses are back-propagated through it.

\paragraph{Size.} The whole network has \MethodParamsTotalL\,M parameters, of which \MethodParamsHeadsL\,M are in the
heads, and needs $\num{\MethodGflopsL}$ GFLOPs for one image with one person (one multiply-add counted as two FLOPs);
the backbone alone needs $\num{\MethodGflopsBackboneL}$. One forward pass takes \MethodMsL\,ms on an A40 GPU. NAVER's
released Anny model has the same backbone and a larger head, \MethodParamsHeadNaver\,M parameters
(\MethodParamsTotalNaver\,M in all).

\section{Losses in plain words}
\label{sec:method-losses}

The ground truth is prepared per person. Anny's $4\times4$ bone transforms become rotation matrices, and the Anny model
runs on the true parameters to give target vertices and joints. The anchor targets (detection token, offset, distance,
translation) are built from one joint; Section~\ref{sec:method-depth} is about which one. The total loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L} = {}& \alpha_{\text{det}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}
  + \alpha_{\text{off}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{off}}
  + \alpha_{\text{rot}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rot}}
  + \alpha_{\text{shape}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{shape}}
  + \alpha_{\text{dist}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}
  + \alpha_{\text{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{t}}
  + \alpha_{\text{j3d}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{j3d}}
  + \alpha_{\text{v3d}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{v3d}} \\
  &+ \mathbb{1}[e \ge e_{\text{2D}}]\left(\alpha_{\text{j2d}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{j2d}}
  + \alpha_{\text{v2d}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{v2d}}\right)
  + \lambda_{\text{kd}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{kd}} + \lambda_{\text{kd-out}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{kd-out}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:method-loss}
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the epoch and the two distillation terms exist only for a student. Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights} says what
each term compares and lists its weight in the code and in the three runs that matter. Every term is an L1 distance
except the detection term and the feature distillation term.

\input{tables/T02_loss_weights}

\paragraph{Detection.} The detection term is the focal loss of CornerNet \cite{law2018cornernet} in the form CenterNet
uses: positives are the tokens that hold a person's anchor joint, and confident wrong scores are penalised most. The
training log calls it \texttt{bce}, but it is not a binary cross-entropy. One token can hold one person; a second
person whose anchor falls into the same token is dropped from the targets of that image.

\paragraph{Rotations and the boost.} The rotation term sums the L1 difference over the nine entries of each bone's
rotation matrix and over the bones. The Base recipe multiplies the weight of the neck, head and hand bones by
\MethodBoost{} and then rescales all weights to a mean of 1. After this rescaling the boosted bones weigh
$\times$\MethodBoostOn{} and all other bones $\times$\MethodBoostOff: the boost moves loss from the body to the neck
and hands; it does not add loss.

\paragraph{Shape.} The shape term compares the \MethodNumPheno{} phenotypes after the sigmoid. Two properties matter
later. An L1 loss is smallest at the median of the possible answers, so when the image gives only weak cues it pulls
the prediction toward the training median. The sigmoid is flat near 0 and 1, so very young or very old values are
learned slowly (Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics}). The Base recipe raised the shape weight from \MethodWShapeDefault{} to
\MethodWShape.

\paragraph{Which terms see depth.} The 3D joint and vertex terms move both skeletons to their own pelvis before
comparing them. They carry no information about where the body is. Only four terms see placement: distance and
translation directly, and the 2D joint and vertex terms, because they project the mesh with the predicted anchor and
depth. Before the depth fix, distance and translation together made up about \MethodLossShareDepth\% of the total loss,
against \MethodLossShareShape\% for the shape term. This imbalance is one reason for the larger depth weight of
Section~\ref{sec:method-depth}.

\paragraph{When the 2D terms start.} The code default switches the 2D terms on at epoch \MethodStartTwoDDefault, so
that poor early predictions do not produce useless reprojection gradients. Final, Control and Student all start from
trained heads and use the 2D terms from epoch \MethodStartTwoD{} at weight \MethodWTwoD. The draft of this thesis said
the final model enabled them later; that was wrong.

\paragraph{Masked terms for 3DPW people.} Every person carries a flag, 1 for AnnyOne and 0 for 3DPW. For people with
flag 0 the rotation and global-rotation terms skip the \MethodHandMaskBones{} finger, thumb and metacarpal bones,
because the SMPL-to-Anny converter never fits them and leaves them at rest (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). The hand mask
removes the rotation terms only; the mesh terms still carry the rest-pose hands. A second, optional mask for the shape
term is described in Section~\ref{sec:method-real}.

\paragraph{Numerical safety.} Training runs in fp32 with TF32 matrix products; mixed precision is off, because it
produced NaN values with the Anny layer. A step whose gradients contain NaN or Inf values is skipped. This happened in
\MethodPilotNaNLow{} to \MethodPilotNaNHigh{} of $\num{\MethodShortSteps}$ steps in the short fine-tunes and in \MethodFinalNaNSteps{} of
$\num{\MethodFinalSteps}$ steps of Final. None of the weights in Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights} were tuned in this thesis;
they are the Base recipe plus the depth weight of Section~\ref{sec:method-depth}.

\section{Training schedule and model names}
\label{sec:method-schedule}

\paragraph{The Base lineage.} Training began from NAVER's public ViT-L checkpoint \texttt{multiHMR\_672\_L\_anny}. Its
head has a different structure from ours, so only the backbone was transferred (with a key-name remapping) and the
heads started from random values. Four stages followed. In the first, the backbone was frozen and only the heads
trained, at a learning rate of \MethodStageOneLR{} according to the training scripts (they were edited after the runs,
so this value is not read from a log). In the second, the last \MethodUnfrozenBlocks{} of the \MethodBlocksL{} ViT
blocks were unfrozen at \MethodFinalLR{} and the helper-bone mask was switched on in training. The third added the
$\times$\MethodBoost{} neck and hand boost, and the fourth raised the shape weight to \MethodWShape. Every continuation
used a learning rate an order of magnitude below the cold-start value, with step decay; a constant cold-start rate on
a partly trained model had produced hundreds of NaN gradient events per epoch. Epoch \MethodBaseEpoch{} of the fourth
stage is \emph{Base}, the starting point of every fine-tune in this thesis. It saw only synthetic images.

\paragraph{The final fine-tune.} Final starts from Base and trains for \MethodFinalEpochs{} epochs of
$\num{\MethodFinalIters}$ iterations with batch \MethodFinalBatch, that is $\num{\MethodFinalImages}$ images, at a
constant learning rate of \MethodFinalLR. The last \MethodUnfrozenBlocks{} backbone blocks and the heads train; the rest
of the backbone stays frozen. The loss weights are those of Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights}. Three changes come on
together: the depth fix with the distance and translation weights at \MethodWDist{} (Section~\ref{sec:method-depth}),
the bone correction (Section~\ref{sec:method-bonefix}), and \MethodMixRatio\% real 3DPW images in every batch with the
hand mask (Section~\ref{sec:method-real}). The \MethodManifestImages{} images of the diagnostic manifest
(Chapter~\ref{ch:data}) are never sampled. Training took $\num{\MethodFinalTrainSec}$\,s on one A100 GPU
(\MethodFinalImgPerSec{} images per second).

\paragraph{The control.} Control has the same start, data, mix, hand mask, steps and learning rate as Final. It keeps
the old pelvis targets with distance and translation weight \MethodWDistDefault{} and has no bone correction. The
difference between Control and Final is therefore the two fixes. The difference between Base and Control is the real
images plus the cost of restarting a fine-tune (Section~\ref{sec:method-eval}). The three changes were tested alone
only in short pilots (Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics}); in Final they are switched on together.

one training run. Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} maps each name to its job, log and checkpoint.

\section{The depth anchor and its fix}
\label{sec:method-depth}

\paragraph{What the model anchors.} The network places each person by the head joint. The detection token, the
offset and the predicted depth all describe the head, the predicted translation is the head's position, and the mesh
hangs from the head (Equation~\eqref{eq:method-placement}).

\paragraph{What the targets were.} For AnnyOne images, the training code built the translation, distance, detection
token and offset targets at joint 0, the pelvis. Every checkpoint before this thesis, Base included, was trained on
these targets. Prediction and target therefore described two different joints, about half a body apart.

\paragraph{What this does.} The network must put its head joint where the pelvis target is. The offset head tried to
close the gap: in a one-batch debug print the predicted offsets were $-\MethodOffsetLow$ to $-\MethodOffsetHigh$
tokens, where a correct offset lies within $\pm\MethodOffsetLegal$. A mesh that hangs from a head placed at the
pelvis is drawn too low in the image. The 2D terms penalise this misfit, and the network can shrink it by moving the
body farther away, where it is drawn smaller. The 3D terms cannot object, because they are pelvis-aligned, and the
direct depth terms were too weak to hold the body in place (Section~\ref{sec:method-losses}). The mismatch itself is
certain; that the 2D terms push the body away is the likely mechanism, which I did not isolate in a separate test.
Figure~\ref{fig:anchor} sketches it.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/tikz/F03_anchor}
  \caption{The depth-anchor bug. The model places each body by its head (left); the training targets were built at the
  pelvis (middle). The 2D terms reconcile the two by moving the body away from the camera, where the same 3D body is
  drawn smaller (right). Bottom: the linear fit of predicted against true pelvis depth for the
  \MethodHoldoutPeople{} people of the AnnyOne holdout (setting V1), for Base and for Final. Schematic; the measured
  scatter is in Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics}.}
  \label{fig:anchor}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The consequence.} On the AnnyOne holdout Base predicts a pelvis depth of
$\MethodBaseSlope\times\text{true}+\MethodBaseIntercept$\,m. Every one of the \MethodHoldoutPeople{} matched people is
placed too far, the absolute pelvis error is \MethodBaseRootErr\,m, and the bodies are drawn at \MethodBaseSize{} of
their true size, with the head \MethodBaseHeadPx{} pixels off. The 3D body itself has the right height
(\MethodBaseHeightPred{} against \MethodBaseHeightTrue\,m). None of this shows in PVE, which aligns the pelvises first,
nor in the real-photo demos, which place each body from its detection box. Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics} gives the
measurements and shows that the earlier models carry the same bug.

\paragraph{The fix.} The fix is one switch in the training code (\texttt{--gt\_center\_joint person\_center}): all four
anchor targets are built at the joint the model anchors, the head. The visibility filter and the rule of one person per
token move with them. Nothing in the network changes. I also raised the distance and translation weights from
\MethodWDistDefault{} to \MethodWDist, because these were the only direct depth terms and carried about
\MethodLossShareDepth\% of the loss. In the 10-epoch pilots the switch alone reached a pelvis error of
\MethodPilotDistOneRoot\,m and the switch with weight \MethodWDist{} \MethodPilotDistFiveRoot\,m; a weight of \MethodWDistPilotMax{} was
only tried for \MethodShortEpochs{} epochs. Final therefore uses the switch with weight \MethodWDist, and Control keeps the pelvis targets
with weight \MethodWDistDefault. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows that the fix moved the offset and the drawn size, not
the slope: Final's fit is $\MethodFinalSlope\times\text{true}+\MethodFinalIntercept$\,m.

\section{The bone correction}
\label{sec:method-bonefix}

\paragraph{What it corrects.} A per-bone dump of the predicted rotations on the holdout (Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics})
showed that two bones of the neck chain are turned half a turn in every one of the \MethodFlippedCkpts{} checkpoints
of the Base lineage: neck02 by \MethodBaseNeckTwo$^\circ$ (median error of its parent-relative rotation) and the head
bone by \MethodBaseHead$^\circ$. The two half-turns cancel, so the head still points the right way (global head error
\MethodBaseGlobalHead$^\circ$) and PVE never showed the flip. The neck skin between them is twisted thin: its girth is
\MethodBaseGirth{} of the true value. The body model is not the cause; every single-joint perturbation of the rig moves
the right body part (Chapter~\ref{ch:diagnostics}). Figure~\ref{fig:neck-chain} shows the chain.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/tikz/F04_neck_chain}
  \caption{The neck chain neck01--neck02--neck03--head. Top: in Base, neck02 and the head bone are each turned about
  half a turn; the turns cancel, so the head points right, but the skin between them is twisted to \MethodBaseGirth{}
  of its true girth. Bottom: one constant rotation per bone turns both back. Girth values on the AnnyOne holdout,
  setting V1; values for the two hand bones of the correction are in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.}
  \label{fig:neck-chain}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{How it works.} For each flipped bone $b$ the network's parent-relative rotation is multiplied on the right
by one constant rotation, $\mathbf{R}_b\leftarrow\mathbf{R}_b\mathbf{C}_b$. If the prediction is
$\mathbf{R}_b=\hat{\mathbf{R}}_b\mathbf{F}_b$ for a constant offset $\mathbf{F}_b$, then
$\mathbf{C}_b=\mathbf{F}_b^{\top}$ restores the true rotation $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_b$. I estimate $\mathbf{C}_b$ as the
rotation closest to the mean of $\mathbf{R}_{b,k}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{b,k}$ over the people $k$ of
\MethodBoneFixImages{} randomly drawn training images, never the holdout. The script selects every bone whose median
error exceeds \MethodBoneFixThr$^\circ$ with a constant offset. For Base it selected four bones: neck02
(\MethodCNeckTwo$^\circ$), head (\MethodCHead$^\circ$), metacarpal1.L, the base of the left index finger
(\MethodCMetacarpal$^\circ$), and finger4-1.R, a bone of the right ring finger (\MethodCFinger$^\circ$). The product is
applied right after the Gram--Schmidt step and before the helper-bone mask, forward kinematics and the losses, so
training, evaluation and demos all see the corrected rotations and gradients flow through the constant. The constants
are stored in the checkpoint's settings and applied again whenever the model is loaded. The correction adds no
parameters. A constant at the output is enough because the HPH runs once and its query starts from constant values, so
no uncorrected rotation is fed back into the network.

\paragraph{Why a loss cannot do it.} The L1 distance over matrix entries has a barrier along a twist: half-way back,
at 135$^\circ$, the distance is larger than at 180$^\circ$ (\MethodLOneHalfTurn{} against \MethodLOneFullTurn{} for one bone), so a partial turn back
raises the loss. For the neck, neck02 and head would have to turn back together. A pilot with a geodesic rotation
loss, which has no such barrier, moved neck02 only from \MethodBaseNeckTwo$^\circ$ to \MethodGeodesicAfter$^\circ$;
the neck boost cannot undo a half-turn either. The correction does it directly.

\paragraph{What it does not do.} With the correction and no training, the global head direction gets worse
(\MethodBaseGlobalHead$^\circ$ to \MethodZeroTrainGlobalHead$^\circ$). The head bone's offset differs from person to
person, so one constant per bone leaves the link between the two corrected bones slightly wrong; the estimation script
warns about such a compensated chain when the predicted global error of a bone rises by more than
\MethodGuardRise$^\circ$. Final therefore fine-tunes with the correction in place. A two-sided correction that keeps
the chain intact was not implemented. The origin of the flip is unknown. The 6D start value of the pose output is
singular and would start a bone turned about its own X axis, a tilt; that fits metacarpal1.L but not neck02's twist.
A second ViT-L model trained from the same backbone with fresh heads did not flip the neck bones but flipped arm and
hand bones instead.

\section{Training with real images}
\label{sec:method-real}

\paragraph{The mix.} A weighted sampler draws on average \MethodMixRatio\% of each batch from 3DPW-train, converted to
Anny (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}; corrected labels, $\num{\MethodTrainThreeDPWImages}$ images with
$\num{\MethodTrainThreeDPWPeople}$ people), and \MethodSynthRatio\% from AnnyOne. A dataset class returns the converted
labels in the exact sample format of AnnyOne, so training and evaluation code run on them unchanged. A 3DPW image holds
about \MethodPeoplePerImgThreeDPW{} labelled people against \MethodPeoplePerImgAnnyOne{} for AnnyOne, so 3DPW people
make up roughly \MethodThreeDPWPeopleShare\% of the supervised people; this share is an estimate from the two averages,
not a count. After each epoch \MethodValThreeDPWN{} images of 3DPW-validation are evaluated. The 3DPW test split is
never used for training. The mix answers requirement 2a.

\paragraph{The hand mask.} The converter fits only the root and the bones that carry a mapped SMPL joint; every
finger, thumb and metacarpal bone stays at exact rest. Without a mask these rest poses would be supervised as truth,
and the network would learn to keep 3DPW hands flat. With \texttt{--mask\_3dpw\_hands 1} the rotation and
global-rotation terms skip these \MethodHandMaskBones{} bones for 3DPW people. The fingers are then learned from AnnyOne
only. The mesh terms are not masked, so the rest-pose hands still enter through the 3D and 2D vertex and joint terms.

\paragraph{The shape mask, tested.} The converter fits height and proportions, takes gender from the 3DPW metadata,
and keeps the other \MethodShapeMaskCount{} phenotypes at constants: age \MethodConstAge{} and muscle, weight, cup size,
firmness and the three ethnicity values at \MethodConstOther. These constants are not labels, yet the shape term pulls
3DPW people toward them. With \texttt{--mask\_3dpw\_shape 1} the shape term ignores the \MethodShapeMaskCount{}
constants for 3DPW people; gender, height and proportions stay supervised. The mesh terms still carry the neutral body
of the label, and the 3DPW test PVE is computed against the same neutral-body meshes, so a masked model can score worse
there. The partial variant (\texttt{--mask\_3dpw\_shape\_names}) masks only the \MethodShapeMaskPartial{} values at
\MethodConstOther{} and keeps the age constant supervised. Final-masked uses the full mask; Final-masked-7 uses the
partial one and had not finished when this chapter was written. I kept the unmasked model as Final and as the
student's teacher, because the masked model gave back body variety but lost the gain in gender and part of the gain
in children on real photos; Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows the trade-off.

\section{Knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:method-kd}

A frozen teacher supervises a smaller student on the same image, in addition to the task loss. Three mechanisms carry
over from the first students to the final one; Figure~\ref{fig:distillation} shows the final design.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/tikz/F05_distillation}
  \caption{Distillation of the Student. The frozen ViT-L teacher (Final, backbone in bf16) and the ViT-B student see
  the same image. A projector maps the student's tokens from width \MethodWidthB{} to \MethodWidthL, and heads copied
  from the teacher read them. The feature loss compares the projected tokens with the teacher's ($T=\MethodKDTemp$);
  the output loss compares rotations, phenotypes and depth of the same people. Shaded boxes train.}
  \label{fig:distillation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Feature distillation.} With teacher tokens $\mathbf{z}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C_t}$ and the tokens
$\mathbf{z}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C_t}$ that the student's heads see,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{kd}} = T^2\cdot \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{z}^{t}_n}{T}\right)
  \,\Big\|\, \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{z}^{s}_n}{T}\right)\right),
\label{eq:method-kd}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the softmax over the channels of one token and $T$ the temperature \cite{hinton2015distilling}. The
factor $T^2$ keeps the gradient size comparable across temperatures. The loss sits right after the backbone and before
any head, so the student is asked to reproduce the teacher's representation, not only its predictions, as in FitNets
\cite{romero2015fitnets}. The teacher receives no gradient.

\paragraph{Head transfer and output distillation.} Teacher and student are the same model class, so every head tensor
has the same name in both. The student copies each head tensor whose name and shape match the teacher's. Both models
run on the true person locations during training, so person $i$ of the student is person $i$ of the teacher without a
matching step, and the student's outputs are pulled toward the teacher's:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{kd-out}} = \alpha_{\text{rot}}\left\|\mathbf{R}^{s}-\mathbf{R}^{t}\right\|_1
 + \alpha_{\text{shape}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\phi}^{s}-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{t}\right\|_1
 + \alpha_{\text{dist}}\left|d^{s}-d^{t}\right|,
\label{eq:method-kdout}
\end{equation}
with the task weights of Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights}, so the depth part carries the weight \MethodWDist. Teacher
outputs are detached.

\paragraph{Warm-up freeze and projector-fed heads.} Copied heads that read through randomly initialised input layers
give confident wrong outputs from the first step, and the resulting gradients damage the copied weights before the
input layers adapt. The copied tensors therefore stay frozen for the first epochs, while the backbone and the input
layers train with Equation~\eqref{eq:method-kdout} as a direct target. The final design removes the random input
layers altogether: a linear projector maps the student's tokens to the teacher's width once, every head is built at
that width (\texttt{--head\_dim}), and Equation~\eqref{eq:method-kd} compares the projected tokens with the teacher's.
All head tensors then transfer, the heads receive inputs of the kind they were trained on, and the feature loss trains
the projector toward exactly that input. The deployed student is backbone, projector and heads.

\paragraph{Throughput.} The frozen teacher dominates each distillation step: a ViT-L at
$\MethodImgSize^2$ pixels runs $\num{\MethodNumTokens}$ tokens through \MethodBlocksL{} blocks. Two changes leave the
student's arithmetic untouched and cut the step time of the ViT-S students on an A100 from \MethodItTimeBefore\,s to
\MethodItTimeAfter\,s: TF32 matrix products, and the teacher's backbone under bfloat16 autocast inside
\texttt{no\_grad}, with its tokens cast back to fp32 before its heads and the loss. The first evaluation after the
change matched the one before it to the decimal.

\subsection{The ViT-B student of Final}
\label{sec:method-student}

\paragraph{Recipe.} The Student follows the S6 design with a larger backbone and the corrected training of this
chapter (Table~\ref{tab:student-recipe}). The teacher is Final, frozen, with its backbone in bf16. The student
backbone is a DINOv2 ViT-B/14 initialised from NAVER's Multi-HMR ViT-B, which was trained for mesh recovery; S4 showed
that such a start beats the ImageNet one. The whole backbone trains. A projector from \MethodWidthB{} to
\MethodWidthL{} (\MethodStudentProjParams\,M parameters) feeds heads copied from the teacher (\MethodStudentHeadParams\,M);
the copied heads stay frozen for \MethodStudentFreeze{} epochs. The targets are exactly the teacher's: head anchor with
distance and translation weight \MethodWDist, \MethodMixRatio\% 3DPW-train on the corrected labels, the hand mask, no
shape mask, the manifest excluded, 2D terms from epoch \MethodStartTwoD. Feature distillation has weight
\MethodKDLambda{} at $T=\MethodKDTemp$, output distillation weight \MethodKDOutLambda. The schedule is
\MethodStudentEpochs{} epochs of $\num{\MethodStudentIters}$ iterations with batch \MethodStudentBatch, that is
$\num{\MethodStudentImages}$ images, \MethodStudentImageRatio{} times the teacher's own fine-tune, at a learning rate of \MethodStudentLR{}
halved every \MethodStudentLRDecay{} epochs. The holdout and \MethodValThreeDPWN{} 3DPW-validation images are evaluated
every \MethodStudentEvalFreq{} epochs. The job script's header mentions colour jitter, but no code in the repository
reads such a setting, so I claim no data augmentation.

\input{tables/T11_student_recipe}

\paragraph{Bone correction of the student.} The copied heads were trained with the teacher's correction: their raw
output for the four bones is the half-turn that the correction undoes. The student therefore trains with the teacher's
correction in place. After training, its own correction is estimated from its raw output on \MethodBoneFixImages{}
training images with the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:method-bonefix} and replaces the teacher's in every evaluation.
If no bone qualifies, the student is evaluated without a correction.

\paragraph{Cost.} The Student has \MethodStudentParams\,M parameters (backbone \MethodStudentBackboneParams\,M, heads
\MethodStudentHeadParams\,M, projector \MethodStudentProjParams\,M), needs \MethodStudentGflops{} GFLOPs per image and
runs in \MethodStudentMs\,ms on an A40, against \MethodParamsTotalL\,M, $\num{\MethodGflopsL}$ GFLOPs and
\MethodMsL\,ms for Final on the same GPU: \MethodRatioParams{} times fewer parameters, \MethodRatioGflops{} times fewer
FLOPs, \MethodRatioMs{} times faster. The heads stay at the teacher's width, so they are a larger share of the
student than of the teacher.

\paragraph{The 95\% rule.} Requirement 1b asks for a smaller model whose error stays within 95\% of the large
model's. I read this as: the student's error is at most the teacher's error divided by \MethodRuleFactor, for PVE and
MPJPE, on both the AnnyOne holdout (setting V1) and the 3DPW test set, and the student has fewer parameters, fewer
FLOPs and a faster forward pass. Against Final the bars are \MethodBarHoldout\,mm holdout PVE and
\MethodBarThreeDPW\,mm 3DPW test PVE. The job re-measures the teacher and computes the bars from those numbers, not
from constants. Against Multi-HMR-Anny the bars would be \MethodBarNaverHoldout{} and \MethodBarNaverThreeDPW\,mm.
NAVER's model was trained on the AnnyOne release, which includes the \MethodHoldoutN{} images we hold out, so its
\MethodNaverHoldoutPVE\,mm is most likely an in-sample number; ours are held out. A student of a teacher at
\MethodTeacherHoldoutPVE\,mm cannot be expected to reach \MethodBarNaverHoldout\,mm on AnnyOne, while the 3DPW bar of
\MethodBarNaverThreeDPW\,mm is above the teacher's own error. Which reference counts is the supervisor's decision; the
thesis reports the student against both (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:method-eval}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} lists the metrics. All errors are means over matched people: a greedy 2D matcher pairs each
labelled person with at most one detection, and recall is the share of labelled people that got one.

\input{tables/T07_metrics}

\paragraph{AnnyOne holdout.} The holdout is the last \MethodHoldoutN{} AnnyOne images (indices
$\num{\MethodHoldoutFirst}$ to $\num{\MethodHoldoutLast}$ of $\num{\MethodAnnyOneSize}$), never used for training.
It contains no children (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). The headline setting is V1: evaluation as in the training code, in
fp32 with the dataset camera, and the helper bones masked as in the demos. Setting V0 leaves the helper bones unmasked
and reads \MethodVzeroGap\,mm higher in PVE on the same people; the draft's headline number was a V0 number. A trace
script adds what PVE cannot see: the pelvis error, the drawn size and the depth fit, all from the model's own
placement, and a dump of every bone's rotation error. Every holdout number in this thesis is V1 unless a caption says
V0.

\paragraph{3DPW test.} All $\num{\MethodTestImages}$ test images with $\num{\MethodTestPeople}$ labelled people of the
corrected labels, evaluated with the same script for every model in setting V0. These numbers are against our
converted labels: MPJPE averages all \MethodNumBones{} Anny joints, and the conversion itself has an error of about
\MethodLabelFloor\,mm (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). They are not comparable with published 3DPW numbers. 3DPW labels only
the actors, so a correct detection of a bystander counts against precision.

\paragraph{Relative Human.} Real photos with an age group (adult, teen, kid, baby), a gender and a box per person, but
no 3D body. I use the validation split, \MethodRHImages{} images with $\num{\MethodRHPeople}$ labelled people, for
evaluation only. Inference follows the demo path on the whole image. Each detection's box is taken from its projected
vertices and matched one-to-one to a labelled box at an IoU of at least \MethodRHIoU. A person counts as child-shaped
when the predicted age is below \MethodChildThr. The child AUC compares kids and babies with adults; gender accuracy
counts a predicted gender below 0.5 as male. All numbers cover detected people only.

\paragraph{Demo images.} \MethodDemoPhotos{} photos and \MethodDemoFrames{} 3DPW test frames, never trained on, are
rendered with the fingers set to rest pose (\texttt{--relax\_hands 1}) and each body placed from its detection box. Box
placement hides depth errors, and nothing on these images is measured: they are judged by eye.

\paragraph{One run, no seed.} The training code sets no random seed, and every model of this thesis was trained once.
Two identical \MethodShortEpochs-epoch runs differed by \MethodNoisePVE\,mm PVE, \MethodNoiseHead$^\circ$ global head direction and
\MethodNoiseRecallPair{} recall points. One run can therefore resolve differences of about \MethodResolvePVE\,mm PVE, \MethodResolveHead$^\circ$ and
\MethodNoiseRecall{} recall points; smaller gaps are called within run-to-run variation. Restarting a fine-tune of Base
with nothing changed already costs about \MethodRestartCost\,mm PVE, so every fine-tune is compared with its own
control, never with Base alone. The pelvis error has no measured noise floor. No bootstrap intervals were computed.
