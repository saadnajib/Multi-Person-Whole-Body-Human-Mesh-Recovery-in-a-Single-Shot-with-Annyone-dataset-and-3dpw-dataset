# Speaker notes: supervisor update, 16 October 2026

## Slide 1: Multi-HMR with Anny: what this week found

Since last Friday I measured each stage of the pipeline separately. I will show what is not the cause, how the 3DPW labels are made, how well they match 3DPW and three defects I found in them, what I found in the neck and a first fix for it, what I measured about the fingers and the children, one depth bug nobody was looking for and its fix, and what is still open. I will say clearly what is measured and what is only a guess.

## Slide 2: Where we started: three visible failures

These are the three failures we discussed last time. Until this week they were explained by guesses: a rig problem, too few pixels on the hands, or a shape head that does not learn age. None of these guesses had been tested on its own. That is what I did first. The last line on each card is what this week showed; the details follow. For the neck, two bones in the v5 models, neck02 and the head bone, are turned half a turn, and the neck skin comes out about half as thick. A first fix, in one run, restores it; holdout renders of the correction alone show normal necks, and on real images the fine-tuned fixed model's necks look normal, judged by eye only; there is no neck metric for real images. For the fingers, about half of the error came from two flipped hand bones.

## Slide 3: What I did: test each stage on its own

I built six checks, each testing one stage. I use a fixed holdout of 100 images that were never used in training, and a fixed set of test cases: 40 training images with adults, children, visible hands and hard necks, and 15 held-out images with no children. Most numbers in this talk come from these sets with the same settings.

## Slide 4: What is not the cause: the structural checks pass

This slide changes the story. The rig, the decoding and the body model are correct, and gradients reach every bone and every shape output. The one exception: two of the three camera outputs are unused and get no gradient. The overfit test shows the optimiser works for shape and 3D joints: on 40 training images the error falls by 36 percent. It has two warnings. First, even while the images were memorised, the translation loss and the 2D losses went up, from 1.67 to 1.89 and from 58 to 75. The network could not satisfy the position targets and the 2D projection at the same time. That was probably the first sign of the depth bug on slide 11; I have not repeated the overfit test with the fix. Second, the rotation loss barely moved, from 44.6 to 40.8. That is a sum over all 75 bones, so it does not isolate the neck; it may be related to the neck flip on slide 7, but that is a guess. So the three failures are not rig or decoding problems, which is how the thesis described them until now, but training can still get stuck. One caveat on the ground-truth check: people who fill the frame lose joints at the image border. That is truncation, not a decoding error. This check covered the AnnyOne labels only; the 3DPW labels come on the next two slides.

## Slide 5: 3DPW to Anny: how the labels are made

One more ground-truth check, now for 3DPW. 3DPW gives, per frame, 24 SMPL joint positions, the camera and the gender. I keep the valid frames and take every third one per person. Then Anny is fitted frame by frame with gradient descent, so that its 24 joints land on the SMPL joints. The two skeletons are different, so first each SMPL joint is assigned one Anny bone; the hip and spine choice is checked on the rest pose, and for nine torso joints, where the rigs differ, the fit aims at a fixed offset from the bone, not at the bone itself. Only positions are matched: SMPL rotations, shape values and the surface are not used. Each person then gets one body, the median over the frames, and the pose is fitted again. Moving into the camera changes only the root bone. When the label files are written, fits with more than 80 millimetres joint error, people less than 30 percent visible and boxes under 40 pixels are dropped. Only the root and 18 bones move. The head, finger and toe end bones are mapped, but nothing below them is matched, so they get no signal. Age is fixed at 0.55. By the code, 144 of the 163 bones stay at rest, including neck02, neck03, the head and the fingers. The check on the next slide confirms this for every person it sampled. So in these labels the head turns only through neck01.

One run, no fixed seed: I claim no PVE gain, only no sign of a PVE cost, 68.8 against 76.9 for the control. The next slide shows the correction alone on a holdout image, and the fine-tuned model on a real 3DPW test frame.

## Slide 10: Children: child-like in training, adult on real photos

I checked in the code that the shape head is complete: all eleven shape values go into the body model and are trained, with no adult default. The model can make a child: for the youngest training people it predicts age 0.10 for a true 0.015, too old but still clearly a child; the holdout adults lie mostly between 0.4 and 0.8. Only 2 percent of training people are children, so I tried a sampler that shows about 10 percent. In a 2-epoch fine-tune nothing changed, not for the training children and not on real photos: five small people still get the age of the holdout adults, 0.57 to 0.67 in the sampler and the control run. That is a short run on few people, so it shows no effect, not that there can be none. My best guess is a gap between synthetic and real images, plus an old-age bias that shows even on training children; neither is tested. The next step is a 10-epoch balanced-sampler run; if that also shows nothing, it goes into the thesis as a limitation. The holdout has no children, so every synthetic child number is in-sample.

If asked: Anny's own age calibration puts 0.10 at about 2 years and 0.67 at about 16, while the labels in our code assume 0.33 is 11 years and 0.67 is 25; I have not settled which one the dataset follows. The ten children in the training manifest also come out too old, by 0.078 on average. With their true shape, their error falls from 47.8 to 41.6 millimetres, so 6.2 millimetres of it is shape. ## Slide 11: The hidden bug: head anchored, pelvis targeted

This is the main result of the week. The model attaches the mesh to the detected pixel by its head joint and reports the head as its position. The training code built the position, distance, heat-map and offset targets at joint 0, the root joint at the pelvis. Those two can never agree. The cause is confirmed: with targets at the head the bodies are drawn much closer to the right size, and a control run with the same schedule does not improve; after 10 epochs its drawn size is 0.59 and its root error 1.44 metres. The likely mechanism is that the 2D losses solve the conflict by pushing the body farther away, so it is drawn smaller. Measured on the holdout for v5: predicted depth is 0.33 times the true depth plus 2.36 metres, all 222 people too far, and the bodies are drawn at about two thirds of their true size in the image, a size ratio of 0.63. Their 3D height is right, 1.405 against 1.409 metres; they are only placed too far away. Our standard error is pelvis-centred, so it never saw this. That is why the thesis numbers looked normal. The real-photo demos did not show it either, because they place each body from its detection box. The synthetic demo and the trace panels use the model's own depth, and there you can see it, as on the next slide. The bug is in the training code, so v9 and v10 have it too: the same kind of depth fit and size ratio, and v10 is slightly worse.

## Slide 13: Depth fix: much better size, near vs far still weak

The fix is one switch: build the training targets at the head, the joint the model anchors. Each fix run has a control with the same schedule and the switch off. The root error is the 3D distance between the predicted and the true root joint, at the pelvis. Drawn size is the predicted head-to-pelvis distance in pixels over the true one; 1 is right. With the depth loss five times stronger for 10 epochs, the root error falls from 1.21 to 0.54 metres, and bodies are drawn at 84 percent of their true size. The controls stay at 1.11 to 1.44 metres. But near and far are still not separated: the slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss. Predictions are pulled toward about 2.5 to 2.9 metres, and I do not know why. The fix likely has a cost: every fix run is worse than its control, by 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres PVE and 1.6 to 2.4 recall points. That is inside or at the edge of pilot noise, so I call it a likely cost, not a proven one. The twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet.

If asked: The switch is gt_center_joint person_center. The stronger-loss runs have no control at the same weight. The mean signed depth offset fell from 1.08 to 0.39 metres, but after the fix near people are too far and far people too near, and these cancel in that mean, so it flatters the fix. The head now lands within about 7 to 10 pixels, as expected, because the target moved to the head. Twenty times for 2 epochs ties with five times for 10 epochs. The pull toward 2.5 to 2.9 metres is close to the estimated training average; holdout people are nearer, 1.9 metres on average, so this pull becomes a large error here. Longer training is not clearly better: with five times, going from 2 to 10 epochs lowered the root error from 0.66 to 0.54 metres but did not improve the slope; with the switch alone neither improved, the root error went from 0.69 to 0.73 metres, and the control also got worse, from 1.11 to 1.44 metres. Root error has no measured noise floor, so I do not read a trend into this. The PVE gaps, 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres, are inside pilot noise; the recall drops, 1.6 to 2.4 points, are at the edge of what one pilot can detect; and the five fix runs share only two controls, so they are not five independent tests. The pelvis 2D error clearly got worse, from about 23 pixels to between 31 and 39 pixels; probably because the body is still drawn too small while the head is now pinned, but I have not checked that.

## Slide 14: Four experiments: results

All four experiments are done, each next to a control with the main switch off. Depth: by its own success rule the experiment failed, because every run, the controls too, misses the limit of 71.5 millimetres PVE and 89.2 percent recall. The 10-epoch run with the stronger loss has 74.8 millimetres and 87.8 percent. The slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss, against 0.33 for v5: far from 1. If asked: The stronger age loss and the stronger depth loss were not separately controlled: both children arms used the same age and height weights, and the depth runs at five and twenty times have no control at the same weight. Depth PVE: 74.8 for the 10-epoch run with the stronger loss, 72.2 for the 10-epoch control, 70.5 for v5. Two identical runs differed by 7.6 millimetres PVE and 1.9 degrees of head error. ## Slide 17: What I claim today, and what I do not

What I claim, measured. The failures are not rig or decoding problems. A training-target bug puts people too far away in v5, v9 and v10; the fix draws bodies much closer to their true size. In the v5 line, neck02 and the head bone sit half a turn off. One pilot run with a fixed correction and a short fine-tune restores the neck thickness and passes the fine-tune checks. Two flipped hand bones were about half the finger error. The 3DPW labels pass on joints and surface, but have three defects. What I do not claim: that the neck fix is proven, since it is one run without a fixed seed; better children; correct depth for near and far people; a depth fix without cost. My question: fine-tune v9 now, or first fix the 3DPW labels and re-fit? I propose the second, as on slide 16, but want your view.

If asked:
- Depth fix: it does not separate near and far people well, and it likely has a cost: every fix run is worse than its control, by 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres PVE and 1.6 to 2.4 recall points. The PVE gaps are inside pilot noise, the recall drops are at the edge of what one pilot can detect, and the five runs share two controls, but all point the same way. In v5 the depth bug affects all 222 holdout people. The ViT-S students use the same training code and are probably affected too; not measured.
- Neck: the two half-turned bones cancel, so the head points the right way. A 2-epoch geodesic loss did not undo it. The fixed correction with a 2-epoch fine-tune did, in one run: neck02 4.7 degrees, thickness 1.00, head direction 14.4 against 13.8 for its control and 12.0 before. One run without a seed is not proof; the holdout renders of the correction without training show normal neck width, and in a first real-image demo the fine-tuned model's necks look normal in the 8 of its 12 images I have seen, judged by eye only; there is no neck metric on real images. The origin of the flip is not known; the singular start value of the rotation output would start a bone turned about its sideways axis, a tilt like the one in v5's left index base, not the twist in neck02 and the head.
- Fingers: two flipped hand bones were about half the finger number, 17.3 to 8.3 degrees; I have not looked at the hands, so I do not claim better-looking fingers.
- 3DPW labels: 6 train sequences, part of a seventh, and one validation sequence had no labels, because their image files were missing on the cluster. On 16 October I re-extracted them, captureSelfies_00 went from 480 to 750 files, and a second rebuild, job 3581320, brought the labels back: train 6,437 label images instead of 4,451, validation 2,777 instead of 2,458. Gender and the missing second person still need the re-fit. For the women, the gender refit is mixed. In the 3DPW labels the head, neck02 and neck03 stay at rest; only neck01 turns. Overall the labels keep about half the head turn, 9.5 against 20 degrees. Train and validation keep most of it; the loss is in the test split, and I do not know why yet. Age is fixed, and the limb-length ratios are not explained yet.
- Option 1, v9 now: the fine-tune is about 1 to 2 hours of training; that is not measured with 3DPW batches, and the v9 script expects 8 to 10 minutes per epoch. From v5 I expect a drawn size of about 0.84 and a root error of about half a metre; on v5 that run cost 2.6 millimetres PVE and 1.6 recall points against its control. Five, not twenty, because the twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet. v9 is best on our 3DPW-Anny labels, but those labels have the gender and two-person defects, so that score is not a clean measure. On the AnnyOne holdout v9 is not the best: 75.0 at epoch 62, the planned start, and 77.0 at epoch 149, against 70.5.
- Option 2, labels first: men to 0, women to 1 by Anny's convention, one shared frame grid, re-fit all 87 conversion tasks into a new folder, rebuild the labels, then fine-tune. Rebuilding the labels alone is not enough, because the gender and the frame choice are fixed inside the fit; the re-fit is an 87-task array job, about 28 GPU hours at the measured 4.4 seconds per frame, so about 3.5 to 4 hours with 8 free H100s. A first test on one two-person sequence, on 17 October, gave the man the correct gender and 14.3 millimetres, with both people on one shared frame grid; the full re-fit has not run yet. The fine-tune after it would start from v5 with v5's own bone fix and the depth fix, run 10 epochs, and have a control run without the two fixes; it has not run yet. I prefer the re-fit first: 3DPW is 30 percent of each batch, so a fine-tune before it would keep learning the wrong body for the men.
- Bone flips in v9 and v10: a v9 fine-tune keeps the neck flip unless it carries the bone fix, estimated on v9 first; the v5 constants belong to v5. The flip was measured on v9 epoch 149; epoch 62, the planned start, was never measured per bone, so there it is likely, not shown. v10 has no neck flip, but the same kind of flip in the left shoulder and upper arm, which is likely most of why its shoulders and arms are worse; not shown.
- Both options: either fine-tune should also mask the 3DPW finger labels, which are set to the rest pose, with mask_3dpw_hands.
