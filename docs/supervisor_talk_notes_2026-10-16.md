# Speaker notes: supervisor update, 16 October 2026

## Slide 1: Title

Since last Friday I measured each stage of the pipeline separately. I will show what is not the cause, what I measured about the neck, the fingers and the children, one bug nobody was looking for and its fix, and what is still open. I will say clearly what is measured and what is only a guess.

## Slide 2: Where we started

These are the three failures we discussed last time. Until this week they were explained by guesses: a rig problem, too few pixels on the hands, or a shape head that does not learn age. None of these guesses had been tested on its own. That is what I did first. The last line on each card is what this week showed; the details follow.

## Slide 3: What I did

I built six checks, each testing one stage. I use a fixed holdout of 100 images that were never used in training, and a fixed set of test cases: 40 training images with adults, children, visible hands and hard necks, and 15 held-out images with no children. Most numbers in this talk come from these sets with the same settings.

## Slide 4: What is not the cause

This slide changes the story. The rig, the decoding and the body model are correct, and gradients reach every output. The overfit test shows the optimiser works for shape and 3D joints: on 40 training images the error falls by 36 percent. But the rotation loss barely moves there, from 44.6 to 40.8, which fits the stuck neck on the next slide. So the three failures are not rig or decoding problems, which is how the thesis described them until now, but training can still get stuck. One caveat on the ground-truth check: people who fill the frame lose joints at the image border. That is truncation, not a decoding error.

## Slide 5: Neck

The neck length is right, 99 percent of the truth. The problem is the rotation of the neck bones. In v5, in v9 and in all six children, neck and finger pilots, the three neck bones are on average 63 degrees off, and head and jaw together average 90 degrees. For v5 I checked every person: the offset is the same, plus or minus about 1.5 degrees, so it is fixed, not noise. The depth runs were not measured for this. Even 149 thousand more training steps in v9 did not change it. v10, which trained its heads from scratch, gets these bones right: 5 and 4 degrees. So the same loss can teach the neck. But v10 is not the answer: its shoulders are 50 degrees off, its arms 36, and its fingers are worse too. v10 also differs from v5 in three ways: it never had the four-times neck boost, its heads started fresh, and it saw 3DPW from the start. So I cannot yet say which of these matters, and large fixed offsets may simply land in different bones in different runs. I do not know the cause yet. My best guess is that the rotation loss compares the matrix numbers one by one and can get stuck near a 180-degree flip. 62.7 is a mean over three bones: one bone flipped by about 180 degrees and two almost right would give it. 89.5 is the mean of head and jaw: one of them flipped would give it. That fits, but I have not tested it. An idea that the rotation outputs were frozen was checked and is ruled out. The head still points roughly the right way, 12 degrees off. Last week I said the long-neck look comes from the depth bug. That is wrong: the neck montage and the real-photo demos place each body from its detection box, so the depth bug cannot reach them. It is likely, but not shown, that this offset is what makes the necks look wrong. v10's neck looks right in the demo montage, but that is one photo, and the head-neck surface error and the head direction are about the same for v5 and v10. The head-neck error cannot see the neck anyway, because 91 percent of its vertices are on the head. The next test is the error of each neck bone, split into twist and swing, an error on the neck skin only, and a trace of v3, which was trained before the neck boost, to see when the offset appeared.

## Slide 6: Fingers

With predicted fingers the error is 17.3 degrees. With the fingers simply left in the rest pose it is 15.1. So the hand prediction is worse than no prediction, which is why the demos zero the fingers and now say so. Hands are small: about two tokens at 672 pixels. I cropped around the person at test time, without retraining. That magnifies the hands by 1.6 times on median, and by 2.5 times or more for a quarter of the people. The finger error does not change: 17.2 against 17.3, and from 17.7 to 17.5 for the most magnified quarter. The body error nearly doubles on these crops, because the model never saw such a camera in training. So test-time magnification does not help; a model trained on larger hands is not tested. A rebalanced loss also changed nothing: 17.5 against 17.4 for its control. That loss cut the hands' share of the rotation loss from about 70 to 33 percent, so it does not test giving the fingers more weight; the earlier four-times hand boost did not help either. My best guess is supervision or model capacity, but that is not tested.

## Slide 7: Children

I checked in the code that the shape head is complete: all eleven shape values go into the body model and are trained, with no adult default. The model can make a child: for the youngest training people it predicts age 0.10 for a true 0.015. On Anny's age scale that is about 4 years instead of about 1.5, so too old but still clearly a child. The ten children in the training manifest also come out too old, by 0.078 on average. Only 2 percent of training people are children, so I tried a sampler that shows about 10 percent. In a 2-epoch fine-tune nothing changed, not for the training children and not on real photos, where five small people still get an adult age of about 0.6, which is about 22 years. That is a short run on few people, so it shows no effect, not that there can be none. My best guess is a gap between synthetic and real images, but that is not tested. There are no labelled real children, and the 3DPW labels fix age at one adult value, so this is a limitation for the thesis, not a fix for this week. The holdout has no children, so every synthetic child number is in-sample. The years assume Anny's default age scale.

## Slide 8: The hidden bug

This is the main result of the week. The model attaches the mesh to the detected pixel by its head joint and reports the head as its position. The training code built the position, distance, heat-map and offset targets at joint 0, the root joint at the pelvis. Those two can never agree. The cause is confirmed: with consistent targets the bodies grow back, and a control run with the same schedule does not. The likely mechanism is that the 2D losses solve the conflict by shrinking the body, which means predicting it farther away. Measured on the holdout for v5: predicted depth is 0.33 times the true depth plus 2.36 metres, all 222 people too far, and bodies about two thirds of their true size, a size ratio of 0.63. Our standard error is pelvis-centred, so it never saw this. That is why the thesis numbers looked normal. The real-photo demos did not show it either, because they place each body from its detection box. The synthetic demo and the trace panels use the model's own depth, and there you can see it, as on the next slide. The bug is in the training code, so v9 and v10 have it too: the same kind of depth fit and size ratio, and v10 is slightly worse.

## Slide 9: Depth fix: before and after

This is what the bug looks like on a synthetic holdout image, using the model's own depth. On the left is the truth. In the middle is the old model: the bodies are too small, the real man's head sticks out above the mesh, and the mesh floats. On the right is the model after the fix, with the depth loss five times stronger, trained for 10 epochs. On this image the meshes cover the people at about their real size; the blue mesh is 95 percent of the true height. I chose this image because it has no people very close to the camera, so it is a good case, not a typical one. On the whole holdout the median body size is 84 percent of the truth, so bodies are still about 16 percent too small, and near and far people are still not separated well. I checked all 12 holdout images we rendered, and all three prediction settings show the same change, not only this one. Both models also miss people who stand very close to the camera and fill much of the image. That is a separate detection problem; the fix did not cause it and did not fix it. The mesh colours follow detection order, so they differ from the ground truth.

## Slide 10: Depth fix: numbers

The fix is one switch: build the training targets at the head, the joint the model anchors. I compared it with a control run that has the same schedule, the switch off and the normal depth weight. In the slope row, 1 means perfect and 0 means everyone gets the same depth. After 10 epochs the head lands within 8 to 9 pixels on average, against 64 for the control; that is expected, because the target moved to the head. The depth error is nearly halved. The control does not improve; it drifts slightly worse, to 1.30 metres. With the depth loss five times stronger, depth error goes from 1.08 to 0.39 metres, and bodies are 84 percent of their true size instead of 63. The remaining shrinkage now shows at the pelvis, 31 to 38 pixels off. But the slope stays at about 0.4: near and far people still get almost the same depth. The stronger-loss runs have no control with the same weight, so that part is read against the plain fix. My best guess is that the model pulls everyone toward a typical training distance, about 2.5 to 2.8 metres, while the holdout people stand nearer, about 1.9 metres. The cost: in every fix run PVE is about 3 millimetres above the control and 4 to 5 above v5, and recall is 2 to 3 points below v5, or 1.6 to 2.4 below the control. A single pilot cannot resolve differences this small, so a small cost is not ruled out. Part of the recall drop may be a counting effect: the test counts people whose pelvis is visible, while training's own head-based count gives 92 to 94 percent. That is not tested. The run with the depth loss twenty times stronger for 10 epochs has no result yet, so I quote no number for it.

## Slide 11: Experiments: results

All four experiments are done. Each ran next to a control with the main switch off, and all are evaluated on the same 100-image holdout and the same manifest, with helper bones masked. The stronger age loss and the stronger depth loss were not separately controlled: both children arms used the same age and height weights, and the depth runs at five and twenty times have no control at the same weight. Depth: the switch alone nearly halves the depth error; with the stronger depth loss it reaches 0.39 metres and a size of 0.84. But the slope is stuck, and PVE did not recover: 74.8 against 70.5. Children, neck and fingers: no measurable effect. The neck loss gave 11.3 degrees of head error, which passes the rule of below 12, but the two control runs gave 11.7 and 13.6, so that is inside the noise, and its PVE and recall guard failed. One warning: there is no fixed random seed, and two runs with identical settings differed by 7.6 millimetres PVE and 1.9 degrees of head error. So one pilot can only detect a difference of about 10 millimetres PVE, 3 degrees of head error or 2 points of recall. Restarting the fine-tune alone costs about 2.4 millimetres. A 2-epoch pilot trains in about 12 minutes, a 10-epoch run in about one hour. No new architecture, because the diagnostics do not justify one.

Six things are still open. First, why the v5 models are stuck on the neck. ## Slide 14: Claims

What is measured: the failures are not rig or decoding problems. A training-target bug puts people too far away in v5, v9 and v10; in v5 it is every one of the 222 holdout people. The fix brings the bodies much closer to their true size, but near and far people are still not separated well, and PVE and recall may be slightly worse. Test-time magnification does not help the fingers. In a short fine-tune, more synthetic children did not help on real photos. The neck is stuck in the v5 models, but v10 learns it, so the cause is still open. What I do not claim: any improvement in the neck, the fingers or the children. My question: should I fine-tune v9, our best model on 3DPW, with the corrected targets and the depth loss five times stronger, before the thesis numbers are final? That is a 10-epoch fine-tune like the pilots, about one hour of training. I expect bodies near their true size, at a possible cost of 3 to 5 millimetres PVE and 2 to 3 points of recall, and I do not know the effect on 3DPW yet. On the AnnyOne holdout v9 is not the best, 77.0 against 70.5 millimetres.
