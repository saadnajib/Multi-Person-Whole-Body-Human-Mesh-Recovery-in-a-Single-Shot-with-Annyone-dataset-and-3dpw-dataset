# Speaker notes: supervisor update, 16 October 2026

## Slide 1: Multi-HMR with Anny: what this week found

Since last Friday I measured each stage of the pipeline separately. I will show what is not the cause, how the 3DPW labels are made and two bugs I found in them, what I measured about the neck, the fingers and the children, one depth bug nobody was looking for and its fix, and what is still open. I will say clearly what is measured and what is only a guess.

## Slide 2: Where we started: three visible failures

These are the three failures we discussed last time. Until this week they were explained by guesses: a rig problem, too few pixels on the hands, or a shape head that does not learn age. None of these guesses had been tested on its own. That is what I did first. The last line on each card is what this week showed; the details follow. For the neck, I found a fixed rotation error in the v5 models, but I have not yet shown that it causes the look.

## Slide 3: What I did: test each stage on its own

I built six checks, each testing one stage. I use a fixed holdout of 100 images that were never used in training, and a fixed set of test cases: 40 training images with adults, children, visible hands and hard necks, and 15 held-out images with no children. Most numbers in this talk come from these sets with the same settings.

## Slide 4: What is not the cause: the structural checks pass

This slide changes the story. The rig, the decoding and the body model are correct, and gradients reach every bone and every shape output. The one exception: two of the three camera outputs are unused and get no gradient. The overfit test shows the optimiser works for shape and 3D joints: on 40 training images the error falls by 36 percent. It has two warnings. First, even while the images were memorised, the translation loss and the 2D losses went up, from 1.67 to 1.89 and from 58 to 75. The network could not satisfy the position targets and the 2D projection at the same time. That was probably the first sign of the depth bug on slide 10; I have not repeated the overfit test with the fix. Second, the rotation loss barely moved, from 44.6 to 40.8. That is a sum over all 75 bones, so it does not isolate the neck; it may be related to the stuck neck on slide 7, but that is a guess. So the three failures are not rig or decoding problems, which is how the thesis described them until now, but training can still get stuck. One caveat on the ground-truth check: people who fill the frame lose joints at the image border. That is truncation, not a decoding error. This check covered the AnnyOne labels only; the 3DPW labels come on the next two slides.

## Slide 5: 3DPW to Anny: how the labels are made

One more ground-truth check, now for 3DPW. 3DPW gives, per frame, 24 SMPL joint positions, the camera and the gender. I keep the valid frames and take every third one per person. Then Anny is fitted frame by frame with gradient descent, so that its 24 joints land on the SMPL joints. The two skeletons are different, so first each SMPL joint is assigned one Anny bone; the hip and spine choice is checked on the rest pose, and for nine torso joints, where the rigs differ, the fit aims at a fixed offset from the bone, not at the bone itself. Only positions are matched: SMPL rotations, shape values and the surface are not used. Each person then gets one body, the median over the frames, and the pose is fitted again. Moving into the camera changes only the root bone. When the label files are written, fits with more than 80 millimetres joint error, people less than 30 percent visible and boxes under 40 pixels are dropped. Only the root and 18 bones move. The head, finger and toe end bones are mapped, but nothing below them is matched, so they get no signal. Age is fixed at 0.55, and about 144 of the 163 bones stay at rest, including neck02, neck03, the head and the fingers. So in these labels the head never turns relative to the neck.

## Slide 6: Conversion: camera verified, fit not checked, two bugs

Short answer: partly. Some parts are measured. On 60 frames of one sequence, raw 3DPW joints land on OpenPose at 8.9 pixels, so the camera formula is right, and the step into the camera is exact. Sides, units and image numbers are right in the code. The fit error is saved for every frame on the cluster and used for the 80 millimetre drop, but nobody has summarised it; the 13 to 20 millimetres in our scripts has no log. An old pilot, not v2.2, matched its own neutral-SMPL target to 13 millimetres on the limbs, but that target was 58 millimetres from the real 3DPW joints, so v2 changed the target; I do not use that number as evidence. A perfect-target test shows why joint error is not enough: joints match to 1.5 millimetres, but a 45-degree head turn is lost. Limb twist is not recovered either. I also found two real bugs. Gender is reversed: men get a female body and women a male one. The code is clear. I also checked Anny directly: at gender 0 the rest body is 1.75 metres tall with 37-centimetre shoulders, at gender 1 it is 1.63 metres with 31 centimetres, so 0 is male. The renders I saw are from v2.1, which uses the same rule, and they show men only. In two-person scenes the frames are picked per person, so at least a third (validation) to a half (train, test) of the images label only one person. Also, 6 train and 2 validation sequences have no labels at all; I do not know why yet. v7 to v10 train on these labels, and our 3DPW scores use them. So our 3DPW scores are measured against these Anny labels on every third frame; they cannot be compared with published 3DPW results, which use the SMPL meshes and all valid frames. I wrote a cluster check that compares the labels with the original SMPL meshes and refits a few people per sequence with the flipped gender; it has not run yet.

## Slide 7: Neck: right length, but the v5 models are stuck

The neck length is right, 99 percent of the truth, and with the true shape it is exact. The problem is the rotation of each neck bone relative to its parent. In v5, v9 and all six pilot runs, the three neck bones are on average 63 degrees off, head and jaw 90. In v5 that is the same for every person, within about 1.5 degrees: a fixed offset, not noise. 150 thousand more steps in v9 did not change it, and neither did a pilot that gave head and neck a third of the rotation loss. v10 gets these bones right, 5 and 4 degrees, so the loss can teach the neck, but v10 is worse elsewhere. Its history differs: v10 was trained with the helper bones masked from the start, with no four-times neck boost and with 3DPW. v5 first trained on a frozen backbone with the helper bones unmasked; masking and the neck boost came later. The head points 12 degrees off on the holdout, 8.6 on the held-out test cases and about 20 on the 40 training test cases. I do not know the cause. My guess: the loss compares rotation matrices number by number and can stick near a 180-degree flip; a neck bone and the head flipped about the same axis would cancel, so the head can still point within 12 degrees. I have not shown that this offset makes the necks look wrong; the head-and-neck error PVE_hn cannot tell, because 91 percent of its vertices are on the head. The depth bug cannot cause the neck look in the montage or the photo demos: they place each body from its detection box.

## Slide 8: Fingers: test-time zoom and a loss rebalance do not help

With predicted fingers the error is 17.3 degrees. With the fingers simply left in the rest pose it is 15.1. So the hand prediction is worse than no prediction, which is why the demos zero the fingers and now say so. Hands are small: about two tokens at 672 pixels. I cropped around the person at test time, without retraining. Per person that magnifies the hands by 1.6 times on median, and by 2.5 times or more for a quarter of the people; 26 percent of the people are not magnified at all. The table compares median hand sizes, so it shows a slightly larger factor. The finger error does not change: 17.2 against 17.3, and from 17.7 to 17.5 for the most magnified quarter. The body error nearly doubles on these crops, because the model never saw such a camera in training. So test-time magnification does not help; a model trained on larger hands is not tested. A rebalanced loss also changed nothing: 17.5 against 17.4 for its control. That loss cut the hands' share of the rotation loss from about 70 percent, which includes the four-times hand boost, to 33 percent; without the boost the share would be 40. So it does not test giving the fingers more weight, and the earlier four-times hand boost did not help either. My best guess is supervision or model capacity, but that is not tested.

## Slide 9: Children: child-like in training, adult on real photos

I checked in the code that the shape head is complete: all eleven shape values go into the body model and are trained, with no adult default. The model can make a child: for the youngest training people it predicts age 0.10 for a true 0.015, too old but still clearly a child; the holdout adults lie mostly between 0.4 and 0.8. Anny's own age calibration puts 0.10 at about 2 years and 0.67 at about 16, while the labels in our code assume 0.33 is 11 years and 0.67 is 25; I have not settled which one the dataset follows. The ten children in the training manifest also come out too old, by 0.078 on average. With their true shape, their error falls from 47.8 to 41.6 millimetres, so 6.2 millimetres of it is shape. Only 2 percent of training people are children, so I tried a sampler that shows about 10 percent. In a 2-epoch fine-tune nothing changed, not for the training children and not on real photos: five small people still get the age of the holdout adults, 0.57 to 0.67 in the sampler and the control run. So I compare with the holdout adults rather than give years: 0.57 to 0.67 is about 14 to 16 years on Anny's calibration and 21 to 25 on our code's labels; either way far too old for people 0.6 to 1.1 metres tall. That is a short run on few people, so it shows no effect, not that there can be none. My best guess is a gap between synthetic and real images, plus an old-age bias that shows even on training children; neither is tested. There are no labelled real children, and the 3DPW labels fix age at one value, 0.55, for everyone (slide 5). The next step is a 10-epoch balanced-sampler run; if that also shows nothing, it goes into the thesis as a limitation. The holdout has no children, so every synthetic child number is in-sample.

## Slide 10: The hidden bug: head anchored, pelvis targeted

This is the main result of the week. The model attaches the mesh to the detected pixel by its head joint and reports the head as its position. The training code built the position, distance, heat-map and offset targets at joint 0, the root joint at the pelvis. Those two can never agree. The cause is confirmed: with targets at the head the bodies are drawn much closer to the right size, and a control run with the same schedule does not improve; after 10 epochs its drawn size is 0.59 and its root error 1.44 metres. The likely mechanism is that the 2D losses solve the conflict by pushing the body farther away, so it is drawn smaller. Measured on the holdout for v5: predicted depth is 0.33 times the true depth plus 2.36 metres, all 222 people too far, and the bodies are drawn at about two thirds of their true size in the image, a size ratio of 0.63. Their 3D height is right, 1.405 against 1.409 metres; they are only placed too far away. Our standard error is pelvis-centred, so it never saw this. That is why the thesis numbers looked normal. The real-photo demos did not show it either, because they place each body from its detection box. The synthetic demo and the trace panels use the model's own depth, and there you can see it, as on the next slide. The bug is in the training code, so v9 and v10 have it too: the same kind of depth fit and size ratio, and v10 is slightly worse.

## Slide 11: Depth fix: bodies drawn much closer to true size

This is the bug on a synthetic holdout image, using the model's own depth, not the box placement of the real-photo demos. Left is the truth. In the middle, the old model: the bodies are drawn too small, the real man's head sticks out above the mesh, and the mesh floats. On the right, the fix with the depth loss five times stronger, trained for 10 epochs. The blue mesh is now 95 percent of the true height; the woman's mesh is closer to her size but still narrower. I chose this image because no person with a ground-truth mesh is missed, so it is a good case, not a typical one; the arm at the bottom left has no ground-truth mesh, so it is not counted as a miss. On the whole holdout the median drawn size is 84 percent of the truth, so bodies are still about 16 percent too small, and near and far people still get similar depths: in the slope, 1 means perfect and 0 means the same depth for everyone. All 12 holdout images we rendered, in all three prediction settings, show the same change. In 4 of them both models miss people very close to the camera; since both miss them, the fix did not cause it. It is likely a detection problem, but not yet counted. Mesh colours follow detection order.

## Slide 12: Depth fix: much better size, near vs far still weak

The fix is one switch, gt_center_joint person_center: build the training targets at the head, the joint the model anchors. Each fix run has a control with the same schedule and the switch off; the stronger-loss runs have no control at the same weight. I report the 3D root error: the distance between the predicted and the true root joint, at the pelvis. Drawn size is the predicted over the true head-to-pelvis distance in pixels. The mean signed depth offset fell from 1.08 to 0.39 metres, but after the fix near people are too far and far people too near, and these cancel in that mean, so it flatters the fix. The head now lands within about 7 to 10 pixels, as expected, because the target moved to the head. With the depth loss five times stronger for 10 epochs, the root error falls from 1.21 to 0.54 metres and bodies are drawn at 84 percent of their true size; twenty times for 2 epochs ties with it, and the controls stay at 1.11 to 1.44. But the slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss. Predictions are pulled toward about 2.5 to 2.9 metres, close to the estimated training average; holdout people are nearer, 1.9 metres on average, so this pull becomes a large error here. Why the model cannot separate near from far, I do not know. Longer training is not clearly better: with five times, going from 2 to 10 epochs lowered the root error from 0.66 to 0.54 metres but did not improve the slope; with the switch alone neither improved, the root error went from 0.69 to 0.73 metres, and the control also got worse, from 1.11 to 1.44 metres. Root error has no measured noise floor, so I do not read a trend into this. Every fix run is worse than its control in PVE and recall. The PVE gaps, 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres, are inside pilot noise; the recall drops, 1.6 to 2.4 points, are at the edge of what one pilot can detect; and the five fix runs share only two controls, so they are not five independent tests. I count it as a likely cost. The pelvis 2D error clearly got worse, from about 23 pixels to between 31 and 39 pixels; probably because the body is still drawn too small while the head is now pinned, but I have not checked that. The twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet.

## Slide 13: Four experiments: results

All four experiments are done. Each ran next to a control with the main switch off, on the same 100-image holdout and manifest, with helper bones masked. The stronger age loss and the stronger depth loss were not separately controlled: both children arms used the same age and height weights, and the depth runs at five and twenty times have no control at the same weight. Depth: by its own success rule the experiment failed. The slope did not move toward 1: it is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 with the five-times depth loss. PVE is 74.8, against 72.2 for the control and 70.5 for v5. What clearly improved is outside the rule: the root error went from 1.21 to 0.54 metres and the drawn size from 0.63 to 0.84. I say that openly instead of changing the rule afterwards. Children, neck and fingers: no measurable effect. The neck loss gave 11.3 degrees of head error, which passes the rule of below 12, but the two controls gave 11.7 and 13.6, so that is inside the noise. The experiments also have a safety limit: PVE at most 71.5 millimetres and recall at least 89.2 percent. The neck run just missed it, with 71.8 millimetres and 89.0 percent, but all five control runs miss it too, mostly because of the restart cost; its PVE is even 4.5 millimetres better than its own control, which is also inside the noise. Its neck bones stayed at 62.5 degrees. For the fingers, the rest-pose floor is 15.2 degrees in this evaluation and 15.1 on slide 8; the difference is only which people are matched. There is no fixed random seed: two identical runs differed by 7.6 millimetres PVE and 1.9 degrees of head error, so one pilot only detects about 10 millimetres, 3 degrees or 2 recall points. Restarting the fine-tune alone costs about 2.4 millimetres. A 2-epoch pilot trains in about 12 minutes, a 10-epoch run in about one hour. No new architecture, because the diagnostics do not justify one.

## Slide 15: Still open, and what I test next

Seven things are still open. First, the neck: the error of each neck bone, split into twist and swing, an error on the neck skin only, and traces of checkpoint 299 and of v3, to see when the offset appeared. Second, the depth slope, stuck at 0.3 to 0.4: why near and far are not separated is unknown. The training depths so far come from small samples, batch means of about 2.5 to 3.2 metres, so I will measure them properly, and measure the fix run's slope on training images; for v5 it was just as low there. Third, the cost of the fix: paired PVE on the same people, recall over people whose head is visible, and the root error next to PVE in the thesis. Testing the fix on 3DPW waits for item seven. The twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet. Fourth, pilot noise: fix the seed or repeat the key run. Fifth, people very close to the camera: both models miss them in 4 of the 12 rendered images; likely a detection problem, not yet counted. Sixth, children and fingers: one 2-epoch test is not enough to close them. Next is a 10-epoch balanced-sampler run, and for the fingers training on larger hand crops. If those show nothing too, both go into the thesis as limitations. Seventh, the 3DPW labels from slide 6: set men to 0 and women to 1, and pick both people's frames on one shared grid. Both happen inside the fit, so all 87 conversion tasks must be fitted again, not only the 11 missing ones, and into a new folder, because the task script skips every person that already has a file; otherwise old and new fits would mix without a warning. Then rebuild the labels and run the new check. Only after that should I test the depth fix on 3DPW or fine-tune on it.

## Slide 16: What I claim today, and what I do not

What is measured: the failures are not rig or decoding problems. A training-target bug puts people too far away in v5, v9 and v10; in v5 it is all 222 holdout people. The depth fix draws the bodies much closer to their true size, but near and far are still not separated well, and it likely has a cost: every fix run is worse than its control, by 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres PVE and 1.6 to 2.4 recall points. The PVE gaps are inside pilot noise, the recall drops are at the edge of what one pilot can detect, and the five runs share two controls, but all point the same way. Separately, the 3DPW labels have the two bugs from slide 6: the two-person problem is counted from the label files, the reversed gender is read in the code and seen in v2.1 renders. I do not claim any improvement in the neck, the fingers or the children, and I do not claim an accuracy for the 3DPW labels: nobody has summarised the saved fit error or compared the labels with the SMPL meshes. My question: should I fine-tune v9 with the corrected targets and the depth loss five times stronger, before the thesis numbers are final? That is about one hour of training. From v5 I expect a drawn size of about 0.84 and a root error of about half a metre; on v5 that run cost 2.6 millimetres PVE and 1.6 recall points against its control. Five, not twenty, because the twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet. v9 is best on our 3DPW-Anny labels, but those labels have the gender bug and the two-person bug, so that score is not a clean measure. The other option is to fix the labels first: men to 0, one shared frame grid, re-fit all 87 conversion tasks into a new folder, rebuild the labels, then fine-tune. Rebuilding the labels alone is not enough, because the gender and the frame choice are fixed inside the fit; the re-fit is an 87-task array job, about 40 GPU hours by the script's own estimate. I think the 3DPW labels should be re-fitted with the corrected gender before any fine-tune that mixes in 3DPW; otherwise the fine-tune keeps learning the wrong body. A v9 fine-tune keeps the stuck neck; v10 learns the neck but has worse shoulders and arms. Either fine-tune should also mask the 3DPW finger labels, which are set to the rest pose, with mask_3dpw_hands. The ViT-S students use the same training code and are probably affected too; not measured. On the AnnyOne holdout v9 is not the best: 77.0 against 70.5.
