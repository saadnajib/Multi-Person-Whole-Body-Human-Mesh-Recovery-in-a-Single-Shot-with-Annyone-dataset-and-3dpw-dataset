# Speaker notes: supervisor update, 19 October 2026

## Slide 1: Multi-HMR with Anny: what this week found

Since last Friday I measured each stage of the pipeline separately. I will show what is not the cause, how the 3DPW labels are made, how well they match 3DPW, three defects I found in them and their fix, what I found in the neck and a first fix for it, what I measured about the fingers and the children, one depth bug nobody was looking for and its fix, the final model with all fixes together next to a control, how it looks on real photos, and what is still open. I will say clearly what is measured and what is only a guess.

## Slide 2: Where we started: three visible failures

These are the three failures we discussed last time. Until this week they were explained by guesses: a rig problem, too few pixels on the hands, or a shape head that does not learn age. None of these guesses had been tested on its own. That is what I did first. The last line on each card is what this week showed; the details follow. For the neck, two bones in the v5 models, neck02 and the head bone, are turned half a turn, and the neck skin comes out about half as thick. A first fix, in one run, restores it; holdout renders of the correction alone show normal necks, and on real images the fine-tuned fixed model's necks look normal, judged by eye only; there is no neck metric for real images. For the fingers, about half of the error came from two flipped hand bones.

## Slide 3: What I did: test each stage on its own

I built six checks, each testing one stage. I use a fixed holdout of 100 images that were never used in training, and a fixed set of test cases: 40 training images with adults, children, visible hands and hard necks, and 15 held-out images with no children. Most numbers in this talk come from these sets with the same settings.

## Slide 4: What is not the cause: the structural checks pass

This slide changes the story. The rig, the decoding and the body model are correct, and gradients reach every bone and every shape output. The one exception: two of the three camera outputs are unused and get no gradient. The overfit test shows the optimiser works for shape and 3D joints: on 40 training images the error falls by 36 percent. It has two warnings. First, even while the images were memorised, the translation loss and the 2D losses went up, from 1.67 to 1.89 and from 58 to 75. The network could not satisfy the position targets and the 2D projection at the same time. That was probably the first sign of the depth bug on slide 11; I have not repeated the overfit test with the fix. Second, the rotation loss barely moved, from 44.6 to 40.8. That is a sum over all 75 bones, so it does not isolate the neck; it may be related to the neck flip on slide 7, but that is a guess. So the three failures are not rig or decoding problems, which is how the thesis described them until now, but training can still get stuck. One caveat on the ground-truth check: people who fill the frame lose joints at the image border. That is truncation, not a decoding error. This check covered the AnnyOne labels only; the 3DPW labels come on the next two slides.

## Slide 5: 3DPW to Anny: how the labels are made

One more ground-truth check, now for 3DPW. 3DPW gives, per frame, 24 SMPL joint positions, the camera and the gender. I keep the valid frames and take every third one per person. Then Anny is fitted frame by frame with gradient descent, so that its 24 joints land on the SMPL joints. The two skeletons are different, so first each SMPL joint is assigned one Anny bone; the hip and spine choice is checked on the rest pose, and for nine torso joints, where the rigs differ, the fit aims at a fixed offset from the bone, not at the bone itself. Only positions are matched: SMPL rotations, shape values and the surface are not used. Each person then gets one body, the median over the frames, and the pose is fitted again. Moving into the camera changes only the root bone. When the label files are written, fits with more than 80 millimetres joint error, people less than 30 percent visible and boxes under 40 pixels are dropped. Only the root and 18 bones move. The head, finger and toe end bones are mapped, but nothing below them is matched, so they get no signal. Age is fixed at 0.55. By the code, 144 of the 163 bones stay at rest, including neck02, neck03, the head and the fingers. The check on the next slide confirms this for every person it sampled. So in these labels the head turns only through neck01.

## Slide 6: 3DPW labels: three defects found and fixed

The finished labels had never been checked end to end against 3DPW. A first cluster check, on 15 October, found that joints, surface and 2D pass, but also three defects: gender was reversed, in 407 of 960 two-person images one person had no label, and eight sequences had no labels or too few. The missing labels came from missing image files; I re-extracted them on 16 October, and a second rebuild brought them back. Gender and the missing person are decided inside the fit, so I re-fitted all 87 people with two fixes: gender by Anny's convention, men 0 and women 1, and one shared frame grid for both people. That finished on 18 October, and the fit's own check passed on all 60 sequences: the men's mean joint error went from 19.0 to 14.7 millimetres, the women stayed at about 15, and the frames where one of two people was missing went from 54 percent to zero. The new labels have 16,694 images with 22,749 people: fewer images than the 18,936 before, with the same people, because both people now share each frame. Then I ran the same conversion check on the new labels, and this slide shows it. Body joints are 14.1 millimetres off at the median, was 18.7, below the about 26 millimetres cited for 3DPW's own joints. The surface is 18.9 and 16.1 millimetres off, was 18.9 and 18.3; that is a nearest-point distance, so a lower bound. In 2D the labels are only 0.4 pixels worse than raw 3DPW, was 1.1. Gender is now right: flipping it makes the fit worse, for the men 13.9 against 20.0 millimetres, where on 15 October the flip was better for 72 of 73. No two-person image misses a person now, 0 of 1,080. The overall grade went from FAIL to WARN. What is left are known differences between the Anny and SMPL bodies, the limb proportions and the hip joint, not label bugs. So the final model can train on these labels.

## Slide 7: Neck: two half-turned bones, and a pilot fix

In all eight v5-line checkpoints, neck02 and the head bone are about half a turn off. The two half-turns cancel, so the head still points the right way and PVE never showed it, but the neck skin comes out half as thick. I tested a fix: one constant turn per flipped bone at the network output, estimated on 200 training images. On the holdout, the fix alone turns both bones back; neck thickness goes from 0.46 to 1.00. But head direction gets worse, 19.1 degrees, likely because the head's offset differs per person. After a two-epoch fine-tune, it passes the fine-tune checks, each within a tolerance of its control; head direction is 14.4, still worse than 12.0 before. One run, no fixed seed: I claim no PVE gain, only no sign of a PVE cost, 68.8 against 76.9 for the control. The final model, 10 epochs with the fix and with real photos in training, keeps it: neck02 4.8 degrees, thickness 1.00, head direction 13.1 (slide 16). The next slide shows the correction alone on a holdout image, and the fine-tuned model on a real 3DPW test frame.

## Slide 10: Children: child-like in training, adult on real photos

On training images the model can make child-like bodies, but on real photos it does not. I measured this on Relative Human, a public set of real photos with age-group labels: 635 validation images with 2,130 labelled people. The detected kids get a predicted age of 0.61 and the babies 0.62, against 0.63 for adults, so age barely separates them: the AUC is 0.61, where 0.5 is chance, and none of the 89 detected kids and only one of the 38 babies falls below our child threshold of 0.45. The model does see that they are small, but it puts that into height, not age: height goes from 0.51 for adults to 0.45 for kids and 0.39 for babies. So it reads children as short adults; in Anny, a low height at an adult age is a short, broad-shouldered adult. Gender carries no signal either: men get 0.535 on average and women 0.551, while Anny's male end is 0. This is the same pull to the training median we see on synthetic data, and it replaces my earlier by-eye reading of a few people in our own photos. The decision for today: write it up as a limitation, or add weak age labels from the Relative Human training set, as the BEV paper did, which is one to two weeks of code after the final model. Separately, a flag can stop the 3DPW labels from training every real person toward one fixed average body; it is off by default, and the final model, trained on 18 October, has it off (slide 16); a run with it on can follow.

If asked: Recall: the model detects only 47 percent of the labelled people, 33 percent of the babies and 43 percent of the kids; the photos are crowded and the model runs at 672 pixels. So every number covers detected people only. Matching: each detection is paired with a labelled box by overlap, IoU at least 0.3. In crowded scenes a few child labels may get an adult detection; that would blur the height drop and any age gap, but it cannot create the height drop, and the clear height drop says most matches are real children. The fine-tuned model with the neck and finger fix gives the same numbers within noise: AUC 0.613, kids 0.62, adults 0.64, gender 0.533 for men and 0.562 for women. That is expected: the fix turns bones, not the shape output. Teens get age 0.60 and height 0.49, between adults and kids in height. Our own photos agree: the one real child, a standing boy, gets age 0.60 and height 0.48; the five small people I quoted before were mostly crouching or seated adults, because the skeletal height I used is measured on the posed body. On synthetic data the same pull shows at both ends: holdout people with a true age of 0.6 to 0.8 come out at 0.57, and the youngest training people at 0.10 instead of 0.015. Showing about five times more synthetic children for two epochs was inconclusive, 48.5 against 53.1 millimetres on ten training children, and it has no mechanism for real photos. So I cannot yet say whether the cause is the gap between synthetic and real images, or the loss, an L1 through a sigmoid on rare values. In Anny a child is mainly narrow shoulders relative to the head, and only a low age gives that; a low height at age 0.6 gives a short, broad-shouldered adult, at least 1.22 metres tall. I do not convert ages to years: our code uses three different scales, and the dataset most likely does not follow Anny's own calibration. Relative Human has about 24,800 people with age-group labels, among them 2,720 children and 1,534 babies, plus gender; its training split is where weak labels would come from. The 3DPW labels fix age at 0.55 and seven more shape values at 0.5 for everyone; only height and proportions are fitted, and gender comes from 3DPW. With the flag on, the final model's 3DPW error can change for a reason unrelated to the depth and bone fixes, because the 3DPW ground-truth meshes keep that average body. Anny-Fit, from the group that made Anny, refits bodies at test time from a vision-language model's age and gender; it could give better figures, but it is not our model.

## Slide 11: The hidden bug: head anchored, pelvis targeted

This is the main result of the week. The model attaches the mesh to the detected pixel by its head joint and reports the head as its position. The training code built the position, distance, heat-map and offset targets at joint 0, the root joint at the pelvis. Those two can never agree. The cause is confirmed: with targets at the head the bodies are drawn much closer to the right size, and a control run with the same schedule does not improve; after 10 epochs its drawn size is 0.59 and its root error 1.44 metres. The likely mechanism is that the 2D losses solve the conflict by pushing the body farther away, so it is drawn smaller. Measured on the holdout for v5: predicted depth is 0.33 times the true depth plus 2.36 metres, all 222 people too far, and the bodies are drawn at about two thirds of their true size in the image, a size ratio of 0.63. Their 3D height is right, 1.405 against 1.409 metres; they are only placed too far away. Our standard error is pelvis-centred, so it never saw this. That is why the thesis numbers looked normal. The real-photo demos did not show it either, because they place each body from its detection box. The synthetic demo and the trace panels use the model's own depth, and there you can see it, as on the next slide. The bug is in the training code, so v9 and v10 have it too: the same kind of depth fit and size ratio, and v10 is slightly worse.

## Slide 13: Depth fix: much better size, near vs far still weak

The fix is one switch: build the training targets at the head, the joint the model anchors. Each fix run has a control with the same schedule and the switch off. The root error is the 3D distance between the predicted and the true root joint, at the pelvis. Drawn size is the predicted head-to-pelvis distance in pixels over the true one; 1 is right. With the depth loss five times stronger for 10 epochs, the root error falls from 1.21 to 0.54 metres, and bodies are drawn at 84 percent of their true size. The controls stay at 1.11 to 1.44 metres. But near and far are still not separated: the slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss. Predictions are pulled toward about 2.5 to 2.9 metres, and I do not know why. The fix likely has a cost: every fix run is worse than its control, by 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres PVE and 1.6 to 2.4 recall points. That is inside or at the edge of pilot noise, so I call it a likely cost, not a proven one. The twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet. The final model on slide 16 uses the five-times fix together with the bone fix and real photos: root error 0.73 metres against 1.40 for its own control and 1.21 for v5, size 0.78 against 0.59, slope 0.42 against 0.28. So its slope is about as good as the best pilots, but still far from 1, and its measured cost is 2 recall points, with PVE inside the noise.

If asked: The switch is gt_center_joint person_center. The stronger-loss runs have no control at the same weight. The mean signed depth offset fell from 1.08 to 0.39 metres, but after the fix near people are too far and far people too near, and these cancel in that mean, so it flatters the fix. The head now lands within about 7 to 10 pixels, as expected, because the target moved to the head. Twenty times for 2 epochs ties with five times for 10 epochs. The pull toward 2.5 to 2.9 metres is close to the estimated training average; holdout people are nearer, 1.9 metres on average, so this pull becomes a large error here. Longer training is not clearly better: with five times, going from 2 to 10 epochs lowered the root error from 0.66 to 0.54 metres but did not improve the slope; with the switch alone neither improved, the root error went from 0.69 to 0.73 metres, and the control also got worse, from 1.11 to 1.44 metres. Root error has no measured noise floor, so I do not read a trend into this. The PVE gaps, 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres, are inside pilot noise; the recall drops, 1.6 to 2.4 points, are at the edge of what one pilot can detect; and the five fix runs share only two controls, so they are not five independent tests. The pelvis 2D error clearly got worse, from about 23 pixels to between 31 and 39 pixels; probably because the body is still drawn too small while the head is now pinned, but I have not checked that.

## Slide 14: Four experiments: results

All four experiments are done, each next to a control with the main switch off. Depth: by its own success rule the experiment failed, because every run, the controls too, misses the limit of 71.5 millimetres PVE and 89.2 percent recall. The 10-epoch run with the stronger loss has 74.8 millimetres and 87.8 percent. The slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss, against 0.33 for v5: far from 1. What clearly improved is outside the rule: the root error went from 1.21 to 0.54 metres and the drawn size from 0.63 to 0.84. I say that openly instead of changing the rule afterwards. Neck and fingers: no measurable effect. Children: inconclusive after two epochs, 48.5 against 53.1 millimetres on ten training children; on real photos, v5 still gives children an adult age (slide 10). The neck loss gave 11.3 degrees of head error, which passes the rule of below 12, but the two controls gave 11.7 and 13.6, so that is inside the noise. With no fixed seed, one pilot only detects about 10 millimetres PVE, 3 degrees or 2 recall points. No new architecture, because the diagnostics do not justify one. The final model, with all fixes together and its own control, is on slide 16.

If asked: The stronger age loss and the stronger depth loss were not separately controlled: both children arms used the same age and height weights, and the depth runs at five and twenty times have no control at the same weight. Depth PVE: 74.8 for the 10-epoch run with the stronger loss, 72.2 for the 10-epoch control, 70.5 for v5. Two identical runs differed by 7.6 millimetres PVE and 1.9 degrees of head error. The slope part of the depth rule only asks for any move toward 1 with a smaller intercept: it passed for the three stronger-loss runs and even for the 2-epoch control, and failed with the switch alone. All runs used the same 100-image holdout and manifest, with helper bones masked. The neck experiment has the same safety limit, PVE at most 71.5 millimetres and recall at least 89.2 percent. The neck run just missed it, with 71.8 millimetres and 89.0 percent, but all five control runs miss it too, mostly because of the restart cost; its PVE is even 4.5 millimetres better than its own control, which is also inside the noise. Its neck bones stayed at 62.5 degrees. For the fingers, the rest-pose floor is 15.2 degrees in this evaluation and 15.1 on slide 9; the difference is only which people are matched. Restarting the fine-tune alone costs about 2.4 millimetres. A 2-epoch pilot trains in about 12 minutes, a 10-epoch run in about one hour.

## Slide 16: Final model: all fixes together, with a control

This is the final model: v5 fine-tuned for 10 epochs with all fixes at once, the depth fix, the bone fix and the corrected 3DPW labels, next to a control with the same recipe but without the depth and bone fixes.
