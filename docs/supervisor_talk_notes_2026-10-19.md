## Slide 3: The model in one picture

This is the model, left to right. The photo is resized to 672 pixels. A DINOv2 ViT-L backbone cuts it into 14-pixel patches, a 48 by 48 grid, and gives one feature per cell. A detection layer says, per cell, whether a person's head is there, with a small offset to place it exactly. For each detected person a cross-attention head predicts the pose, 163 bone rotations of which 75 are trained and 88 helper bones are held fixed, the shape, 11 Anny values between 0 and 1, and the distance from the camera. The Anny body model turns pose and shape into a mesh. What is ours is marked in teal: the output layer sized for Anny, the head-anchor fix in the training code, the bone fix at the output, and the 3DPW conversion and recipe. I want to be honest about requirement 1b: this is still Multi-HMR's architecture, and the backbone weights come from NAVER's Anny checkpoint. The smaller model on slide 24 is my route.

## Slide 4: The datasets

Four datasets, and every number later names one of them. AnnyOne is synthetic: 579,960 renders with exact Anny pose and all eleven shape values, from babies to old people. It is 70 percent of every training batch, and its last 100 images are the holdout that no model trained on. 3DPW is real video, 60 sequences, with SMPL ground truth that I fitted to Anny. Its labels carry pose, gender, height and proportions, but eight shape values are constants and the hands and head stay at rest, so it tests pose more than shape. The train split is 30 percent of each batch; the test split, 8,190 images with 11,854 people, is our real-video test. Relative Human is real photos with an age group and a gender per person and no 3D, so we use it only to measure children, gender and recall: 635 images, 2,130 people. The demo photos have no labels and are judged by eye.

## Slide 5: 3DPW to Anny: how the labels are made

One more ground-truth check, now for 3DPW. 3DPW gives, per frame, 24 SMPL joint positions, the camera and the gender. I keep the valid frames and take every third one per person. Then Anny is fitted frame by frame with gradient descent, so that its 24 joints land on the SMPL joints. The two skeletons are different, so first each SMPL joint is assigned one Anny bone; the hip and spine choice is checked on the rest pose, and for nine torso joints, where the rigs differ, the fit aims at a fixed offset from the bone, not at the bone itself. Only positions are matched: SMPL rotations, shape values and the surface are not used. Each person then gets one body, the median over the frames, and the pose is fitted again. Moving into the camera changes only the root bone. When the label files are written, fits with more than 80 millimetres joint error, people less than 30 percent visible and boxes under 40 pixels are dropped. Only the root and 18 bones move. The head, finger and toe end bones are mapped, but nothing below them is matched, so they get no signal. Age is fixed at 0.55. By the code, 144 of the 163 bones stay at rest, including neck02, neck03, the head and the fingers. The check on the next slide confirms this for every person it sampled. So in these labels the head turns only through neck01.

## Slide 7: What the model learns: the losses in plain words

Training minimises a weighted sum of ten terms, and this table says what each one compares in plain words. Two find the people: a detection score per grid cell, and a small offset that places the head exactly. Four describe the person: bone rotations, the eleven shape values, the distance from the camera, and the 3D position. Four compare the resulting body with the truth: joints and mesh points in 3D, both centred on the pelvis, and the same projected onto the photo. The last column is the weight in the final model, with Multi-HMR's default where ours differs: shape is twenty instead of one, the two depth terms are five instead of one, that is the depth fix, and the 2D terms are small. For 3DPW people the hand bones are not scored, because their labels are a rest pose, not real fingers. The masked arm on slide 19 also drops the eight constant shape values.

## Slide 8: What we changed in training: v5 → final model

The final model is v5 fine-tuned for ten epochs with five changes. First, the depth fix: the training targets for position and distance are built at the head, which is where the model anchors each body, and the two depth losses are five times stronger. Second, the bone fix: four bones are turned back by a constant at the output, with no new parameters. Third, thirty percent of every batch are real 3DPW frames with the corrected labels. Fourth, the hand losses are not scored for 3DPW people, because their finger labels are a rest pose. Fifth, the schedule: forty thousand images, about seventy minutes on one GPU. The control shares everything except the depth and bone fixes, so the right column shows what those two add: bodies drawn at 78 instead of 59 percent of their size, a normal neck, half the finger error, and about two millimetres on the 3DPW test. Each arm ran once, so small differences are noise.

## Slide 9: How we measure

Before the findings, the metrics, in plain words. PVE is the mean distance between predicted and true mesh points after lining up the pelvis; it is our headline number, and lower is better. PA-PVE also matches rotation and scale, so it measures pose and shape alone. Recall is the share of true people found, precision the share of detections that match a real person. The root error is the 3D distance between the predicted and the true pelvis, mostly a depth error. The drawn size ratio says how large the body is on the photo compared with the truth; one is right. The depth slope says whether near and far people are told apart; one is perfect, zero means everyone at the same depth. Bone angle errors near 180 degrees mean a flipped bone. The neck girth ratio compares neck thickness. On Relative Human we report the children AUC and gender accuracy, where 0.5 and 50 percent are chance. And the noise floor: two identical runs differed by 7.6 millimetres, so one run only detects about ten.

## Slide 11: Checked first: what is NOT the cause

Before changing anything I tested each stage on its own, with six checks. Render the labels without the network: do they sit on the people? Move one joint of the body model at a time: does the mesh react correctly? Do all outputs receive gradients? Compare predictions with the truth on a fixed holdout of 100 images and a fixed set of 55 adults, children, hands and hard necks. Put the true shape into the predicted pose. And an overfit test: can the optimiser memorise 40 images? The results below: the ground truth decodes correctly, the body model is fine, and gradients reach every bone and every shape output. The optimiser works, with two warnings: while the images were memorised, the position and 2D losses went up instead of down, possibly the first sign of the depth bug, although that is a guess, and the summed rotation loss barely moved. So the failures are not rig or decoding problems, which is how the thesis described them until now. The cause had to be in training.

## Slide 12: The hidden depth bug: anchor vs target

This is the main result of the week. The model attaches the mesh to the detected pixel by its head joint and reports the head as its position. The training code built the position, distance, heat-map and offset targets at joint 0, the root joint at the pelvis. Those two can never agree. The cause is confirmed: with targets at the head the bodies are drawn much closer to the right size, and a control run with the same schedule does not improve; after 10 epochs its drawn size is 0.59 and its root error 1.44 metres. The likely mechanism is that the 2D losses solve the conflict by pushing the body farther away, so it is drawn smaller. Measured on the holdout for v5: predicted depth is 0.33 times the true depth plus 2.36 metres, all 222 people too far, and the bodies are drawn at about two thirds of their true size in the image, a size ratio of 0.63. Their 3D height is right, 1.405 against 1.409 metres; they are only placed too far away. Our standard error is pelvis-centred, so it never saw this. That is why the thesis numbers looked normal. The real-photo demos did not show it either, because they place each body from its detection box. The synthetic demo and the trace panels use the model's own depth, and there you can see it, as on the next slide. The bug is in the training code, so v9 and v10 have it too: the same kind of depth fit and size ratio, and v10 is slightly worse.

## Slide 14: Depth fix: the numbers

The fix is one switch: build the training targets at the head, the joint the model anchors. Each fix run has a control with the same schedule and the switch off. The root error is the 3D distance between the predicted and the true root joint, at the pelvis. Drawn size is the predicted head-to-pelvis distance in pixels over the true one; 1 is right. With the depth loss five times stronger for 10 epochs, the root error falls from 1.21 to 0.54 metres, and bodies are drawn at 84 percent of their true size. The controls stay at 1.11 to 1.44 metres. But near and far are still not separated: the slope is 0.30 with the switch alone and 0.38 to 0.44 with the stronger depth loss. Predictions are pulled toward about 2.5 to 2.9 metres, and I do not know why. The fix likely has a cost: every fix run is worse than its control, by 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres PVE and 1.6 to 2.4 recall points. That is inside or at the edge of pilot noise, so I call it a likely cost, not a proven one. The twenty-times 10-epoch run has no result yet. The final model on slide 20 uses the five-times fix together with the bone fix and real photos: root error 0.73 metres against 1.40 for its own control and 1.21 for v5, size 0.78 against 0.59, slope 0.42 against 0.28. So its slope is about as good as the best pilots, but still far from 1, and its measured cost is 2 recall points, with PVE inside the noise.

If asked: The switch is gt_center_joint person_center. The stronger-loss runs have no control at the same weight. The mean signed depth offset fell from 1.08 to 0.39 metres, but after the fix near people are too far and far people too near, and these cancel in that mean, so it flatters the fix. The head now lands within about 7 to 10 pixels, as expected, because the target moved to the head. Twenty times for 2 epochs ties with five times for 10 epochs. The pull toward 2.5 to 2.9 metres is close to the estimated training average; holdout people are nearer, 1.9 metres on average, so this pull becomes a large error here. Longer training is not clearly better: with five times, going from 2 to 10 epochs lowered the root error from 0.66 to 0.54 metres but did not improve the slope; with the switch alone neither improved, the root error went from 0.69 to 0.73 metres, and the control also got worse, from 1.11 to 1.44 metres. Root error has no measured noise floor, so I do not read a trend into this. The PVE gaps, 2.6 to 4.9 millimetres, are inside pilot noise; the recall drops, 1.6 to 2.4 points, are at the edge of what one pilot can detect; and the five fix runs share only two controls, so they are not five independent tests. The pelvis 2D error clearly got worse, from about 23 pixels to between 31 and 39 pixels; probably because the body is still drawn too small while the head is now pinned, but I have not checked that.

## Slide 19: Masked shape labels: a trade-off

This slide is about requirement 1a-shape, that body shape should visibly change per person. The 3DPW labels carry eight constant shape values for everyone, because the fitter never estimates them: age 0.55 and seven values at 0.5. The final model learned those constants, so on real photos muscle, weight, cup size and firmness all sit near 0.5 with almost no spread. The mask says: do not learn these eight values from 3DPW people. One run with the mask, otherwise identical, shows a trade-off.
